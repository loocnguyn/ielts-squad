C: Our mountain rescue team was called out a hundred and twelve times last year. You might imagine most of those were climbers on steep rock. C: The second most common problem is getting lost when the cloud comes down. It can happen in minutes. So please carry a paper map and a compass, and know how to use them. A phone is useful, but batteries die quickly in the cold, and in most of the valleys there's no signal at all.
C: Let me talk about the weather. The forecast in the visitor centre is updated at seven every morning. Read the mountain forecast, not the general one. C: What should you take? Waterproofs, always, even on a fine day. An extra warm layer. A hat and gloves. Plenty of food, and at least a litre of water each. And a torch, because walks take longer than people expect, and it's dark by five at this time of year.

C: Before you leave, tell someone your route and when you expect to be back. You can fill in a card at the desk here. If you haven't returned, that's how we know where to start looking.
C: If something does go wrong, stay where you are. Call the emergency number and ask for police, then mountain rescue. If you have no signal, the recognised distress call is six blasts on a whistle, repeated every minute. Keep the injured person warm, and don't give them anything to eat.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about languages that are in danger of disappearing.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: There are roughly seven thousand languages spoken in the world today. By the end of this century, on current trends, at least half will have fallen silent. In this lecture, I'll consider why languages die, why it matters, and whether anything can be done.
C: First, the distribution is extremely uneven. A handful of languages, Mandarin, Spanish, English and a few others, are spoken by half of humanity. At the other extreme, about a third of all languages have fewer than a thousand speakers. The island of New Guinea alone has more than eight hundred, in a population smaller than that of London.
C: Languages rarely die because their speakers die. They die because parents stop passing them on. And parents generally make that choice for a practical reason. They believe that a major language will give their children a better chance of finding work. In many countries, the process was pushed along by schools, where children were punished for using their mother tongue.
C: A language is usually considered endangered when children no longer learn it at home. After that, it can vanish within two generations.
C: Why should we care? One reason is knowledge. Small languages often contain detailed information about local plants and animals that science hasn't recorded. A second is that each language shows a different way in which the human mind can organise experience. Some have no words for left and right, and their speakers use compass directions all the time. They always know which way north is. A third reason, and for communities the most important, is identity.
C: Can a language be brought back? There are encouraging examples. Hebrew had not been used in daily conversation for nearly two thousand years. It survived in religion, and was revived as a spoken language in the late nineteenth century. Today it has nine million speakers.
C: Welsh is a different kind of success. By the nineteen sixties, it was in steep decline. What turned it round was a campaign that won schools teaching in Welsh, and a television channel. The number of young speakers is now rising.
C: In New Zealand, the Maori community set up what they called language nests. These were nurseries where very small children spent the day with grandparents who spoke only Maori. The idea has since been copied in Hawaii and elsewhere.
C: Technology helps as well. Linguists now record the last speakers on video, and communities build dictionaries on their phones. But a recording isn't a living language. In the end, a language survives only if children use it with one another when no adult is listening.

## 4 | You will hear part of a history lecture about the trade routes known as the Silk Road.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: Few names in history are as evocative as the Silk Road. It suggests a single highway, with caravans of camels plodding from China to Rome. The reality was rather different, and today I'd like to correct a few misunderstandings.
C: To begin with, the name itself is modern. Nobody who travelled the routes ever used it. It was invented in eighteen seventy-seven by a German geographer. And there was never one road. There was a shifting network of tracks across deserts and mountain passes, together with sea routes through the Indian Ocean, which eventually carried far more goods than the land.
C: Second, almost nobody travelled the whole distance. Goods moved in stages, passing from one merchant to another at the great market towns, such as Samarkand. A roll of silk might change hands a dozen times, and its price rose at each exchange.
C: The people who dominated this business for centuries were not Chinese or Roman but a people called the Sogdians, from the region of modern Uzbekistan. Their language became the common language of the caravans.
C: And silk was far from the only cargo. Travelling west went paper, porcelain and tea. Travelling east went glass, silver, wine and, above all, horses, which the Chinese valued enormously for their army.
C: The animal that made it possible was the camel, and specifically the two-humped camel of central Asia. It can carry two hundred kilos, go for a week without water, and, according to Chinese writers, sense a sandstorm before it arrives.
C: Far more important than any goods, however, were the ideas that travelled with them. Buddhism spread from India to China along these routes. So did techniques, the making of paper going west, and of glass going east. Crops moved too. The peach and the orange came to Europe from China, and grapes travelled the other way.
C: Not everything that travelled was welcome. Most historians believe that the plague which killed perhaps a third of Europe's population in the fourteenth century was carried along the same routes.
C: What ended the overland trade? There was no single moment. But after fourteen ninety-eight, when Portuguese ships reached India by sailing round Africa, the sea became cheaper and safer, and the cities of central Asia slowly declined.
C: I'll end with a thought. We speak of globalisation as though it were new. The Silk Road is a reminder that people, goods and beliefs have been crossing continents for two thousand years.
