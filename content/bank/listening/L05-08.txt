## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the psychology of waiting in queues.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: This afternoon I'd like to talk about something we all do and nearly all dislike, which is waiting in a queue. It turns out that how long a wait feels has surprisingly little to do with how long it actually is.
C: Let's begin with a basic finding. When researchers time people in a queue and then ask them how long they waited, the estimates are consistently too high. On average, people believe they have waited about a third longer than they really have.
C: The first principle is that time passes more slowly when we have nothing to do. A famous example comes from an office building in New York, where tenants complained about slow lifts. Speeding up the lifts would have been expensive. Instead, the manager had mirrors fitted beside the lift doors. People now had something to look at, namely themselves, and the complaints almost stopped.
C: An airport in Texas found a similar solution. Passengers were complaining about the wait for their bags. So the airport moved the arrival gates further away. Passengers now had to walk for six minutes before reaching the hall, the bags were already there when they arrived, and everyone was satisfied, although the total time had not changed at all.
C: The second principle is that uncertainty makes a wait feel longer. That's why theme parks put up signs showing the waiting time. And notice that the time shown is deliberately too long. If the sign says forty minutes and you reach the front in thirty, you feel that you've gained something.
C: In the same way, people tolerate a delay much better when they are given an explanation. A pilot who tells passengers why the plane isn't moving gets far fewer complaints than one who says nothing.
C: Third, fairness. Nothing makes people angrier than seeing someone who arrived later being served first. This is why banks and airports have moved to one winding line feeding several desks. Interestingly, people accept exceptions when they understand the reason. In a hospital emergency department, nobody objects when a patient whose need is urgent goes straight in.
C: Fourth, and this comes from research on memory, we don't judge an experience by its average. What matters most is the end. A queue that crawls and then suddenly speeds up is remembered far more kindly than one that starts quickly and then stops.
C: Businesses apply all this. Supermarkets put magazines beside the tills, partly to sell them, of course, but also to keep us occupied. And telephone helplines now tell callers their position in the queue, because knowing you are third is far easier to bear than silence.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about how different animals sleep.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: Good afternoon. Today's subject is sleep, and in particular the remarkable variety of ways in which animals do it. Every animal that has been studied carefully turns out to sleep in some form, which suggests that sleep does something essential. Yet the way it's done differs enormously.
C: Take the amount. A large bat may sleep for nineteen hours a day, while a giraffe manages on less than two. The best predictor is not the size of the brain, as was once thought, but diet. Animals that eat plants must spend most of the day feeding, and so they have little time left for sleep, whereas meat eaters, such as lions, can afford long rests after a meal.
C: Then there's the question of safety. A sleeping animal is an easy target. Horses deal with this by sleeping on their feet, thanks to a locking mechanism in the legs. They do need to lie down for the deepest stage of sleep, but only for a few minutes at a time.
C: Dolphins face a different difficulty. They have to come to the surface to breathe, and they do that consciously. If a dolphin slept as we do, it would drown. Its solution is to rest one half of the brain at a time. The other half stays alert, and one eye remains open.
C: Some birds do the same thing. Ducks sleeping in a row are a nice example. Those at the ends of the row keep the outward eye open, watching for predators, while those in the middle sleep with both eyes closed.
C: Birds that migrate raise an even stranger problem. The frigatebird may stay in the air over the ocean for two months without landing. Recordings of its brain show that it does sleep while flying, but for only about forty minutes a day, in bursts of a few seconds. On land, the same bird sleeps for twelve hours.
C: What about much simpler animals? Fruit flies have been shown to sleep, and if they are kept awake, they sleep longer afterwards, exactly as we do. Even jellyfish, which have no brain at all, become less active at night.
C: So why do we sleep? One influential idea is that sleep allows the brain to clear away waste produced during the day. Another is that it's needed to fix memories. Young animals, which are learning fastest, sleep the most.
C: I'll finish with a warning about method. Almost everything we know comes from animals in zoos and laboratories. When sloths were finally studied in the wild, they slept for under ten hours, not sixteen. Captive animals, with nothing to fear and nothing to find, may simply be bored.
