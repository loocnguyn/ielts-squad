## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the history of paper.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: We're often told that we live in a paperless age, and yet the world uses more paper today than at any time in history. This morning I'd like to follow the story of this very ordinary, very remarkable material.
C: Before paper, people wrote on whatever was available. In Egypt, it was papyrus, made from a reed that grew along the Nile. In Europe, it was parchment, made from the skins of sheep and calves. A single large Bible might require the skins of two hundred animals, which tells you why books were so rare.
C: Paper was invented in China. The traditional date is the year one hundred and five, and the credit goes to an official at the imperial court named Cai Lun, although examples have been found that are two centuries older. Early Chinese paper was made from old rags, fishing nets and the bark of the mulberry tree, all beaten to a pulp in water. A fine screen was dipped into the pulp, and the thin layer of fibres left on it dried into a sheet.
C: Its first uses weren't for writing at all. It was used for wrapping and for padding, to protect delicate objects such as bronze mirrors.
C: The secret stayed in East Asia for six hundred years. According to tradition, it travelled west after a battle in seven fifty-one, in central Asia, when Chinese paper makers were taken prisoner. Within decades, there was a paper mill in Baghdad. And paper transformed the Islamic world. It was far cheaper than parchment, and there was a further advantage that rulers appreciated. Ink soaks into paper, so a document couldn't be altered without leaving a trace. That made paper ideal for official records.
C: Paper reached Spain in the eleventh century and spread slowly north. Europeans improved the process by using water wheels to drive the hammers that beat the rags.
C: Then, around fourteen fifty, came the printing press, and demand exploded. For three hundred years, the limit on paper making was the supply of rags. There are accounts of rags being so scarce in England that a law required the dead to be buried in wool, in order to save linen for the mills.
C: The answer came from watching insects. A French scientist noticed that wasps build their nests by chewing wood into a kind of paper. It took another century to turn that idea into an industrial process, but by the eighteen fifties, paper was being made from wood pulp. It became cheap, and with cheap paper came the mass newspaper and the paperback book.
C: There was a cost. Wood-pulp paper is acidic, and it slowly destroys itself. Libraries are full of books from that period that are crumbling to dust.

## 2 | You will hear a guide starting a tour of a chocolate factory.
@C Guide male_us
C: Good afternoon, and welcome to the Hadley Chocolate Works. I'm Marco, and I've been making chocolate here for eleven years. The tour lasts about an hour, and yes, there is tasting at the end.
C: Before we go in, I must ask you to put on a hairnet and a white coat. You'll also need to remove any jewellery from your hands and wrists. That isn't about safety around the machines. It's about hygiene. A lost ring in a batch means throwing away half a tonne of chocolate.
C: A little history. The company was started in nineteen twenty-three by two brothers who'd been selling cocoa as a drink. During the war, when sugar was rationed, the factory almost closed, and it survived by making soap. We don't mention that on the wrappers.
C: Now, where does chocolate come from? It begins as the seed of a tree that only grows within twenty degrees of the equator. Ours come from Ghana and Ecuador. Inside each fruit, or pod, there are about forty beans, and it takes the beans from a whole pod to make one of our small bars.
C: At the farm, the beans are fermented for six days under banana leaves. It's this stage that creates the flavour. A raw bean tastes bitter and nothing like chocolate. They're then dried in the sun and shipped to us in sacks.
C: Here's what you'll see inside. First, the beans are roasted, for about half an hour. You'll notice the smell before you see anything.
C: Next, a machine cracks the beans and blows away the shells. What's left are small pieces called nibs. The shells don't go to waste. We sell them to garden centres, where they're used on flower beds.
C: The nibs are ground between heavy rollers until they turn into a thick liquid. At this point, we add sugar, and milk powder for milk chocolate.
C: Then comes a stage called conching, and it's the one that separates good chocolate from ordinary. The mixture is stirred continuously for up to three days. That makes it smooth and removes any sour taste.
C: After that, the chocolate is cooled and warmed again very precisely. Without that step, the bar would be dull and crumbly. Done properly, it's shiny, and it breaks with a clean snap.
C: Finally, it's poured into moulds, shaken to remove bubbles, cooled, and wrapped.
C: Two requests. Please stay behind the blue line on the floor. And photographs are fine everywhere apart from the moulding room, where the shapes for next season are still secret.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about plastic in the oceans.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: Each year, somewhere between eight and twelve million tonnes of plastic enter the sea. That's roughly a lorry load every minute. In today's lecture, I'd like to look at where it comes from, where it goes, and what might be done.
C: Let's begin with sources. You might suppose that most of it is thrown from ships. That was true fifty years ago. Today, about four fifths comes from the land, and it reaches the sea mainly by way of rivers. A small number of rivers, most of them in Asia, carry a very large share of the total, and that isn't because people there use more plastic. It's because the systems for collecting waste haven't kept up with the growth of cities.
C: What happens to it once it's in the sea? Some sinks. Some washes up on beaches. And some is carried by currents into enormous slowly rotating systems called gyres. The best known is in the North Pacific, and it's often described as an island of rubbish twice the size of France. That description is misleading. You couldn't walk on it. For the most part, you can't even see it. It's more like a thin soup of small fragments.
C: Those fragments are the real concern. Plastic doesn't rot. Sunlight and waves simply break it into smaller and smaller pieces, known as microplastics. And there's a second source, which surprises people. Every time we wash clothes made of synthetic fabric, thousands of tiny fibres go down the drain. Another is the dust worn from car tyres.
C: The effects on wildlife are well documented. Turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish. Seabirds feed bottle tops to their chicks, which then starve with full stomachs. Microplastics are eaten by the smallest animals in the sea and pass up the food chain. They've been found in fish on sale in markets, in sea salt, and in drinking water. What that means for human health, we simply don't know yet.
C: So what can be done? There's a great deal of publicity for machines that would sweep the oceans clean. I'm sceptical. The quantity collected so far is tiny, and the nets catch living creatures as well. Most researchers agree that the priority is to stop plastic reaching the sea in the first place.
C: That means better waste collection in the countries where the rivers are. It means designing packaging that can actually be recycled. Only about nine per cent of all the plastic ever made has been. And it means using less. Bans on plastic bags have cut the number found on beaches in several countries by more than half.
