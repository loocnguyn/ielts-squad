## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the history of glass.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: Look around this room and you'll see glass everywhere, in the windows, in your phones, in my spectacles. Yet for most of history it was rare and precious. Today I want to trace how that changed.
C: Glass is made by melting sand with one or two other ingredients, and nature occasionally does this by itself. Volcanoes produce a black glass called obsidian, and Stone Age people valued it for making blades, because it gives an edge sharper than steel.
C: The first glass made by people comes from the Middle East, about four and a half thousand years ago, and it was used for beads. For many centuries it remained a luxury, as costly as precious stones.
C: The great change came about two thousand years ago, on the coast of what is now Syria and Lebanon, with the invention of glass blowing. A worker picks up a lump of molten glass on the end of an iron tube and blows air into it. Suddenly a bottle that had taken days could be made in minutes. The Romans spread the method across their empire, and for the first time ordinary families could own a glass cup.
C: The Romans also made the first window glass, though it was thick and cloudy. It let in light, but you couldn't see through it clearly.
C: In the Middle Ages, the finest glass came from Venice. In twelve ninety-one, the city ordered all its glass makers to move to the island of Murano. The official reason was the danger of fire in a city built of wood. A happy side effect was that the makers' secrets could be guarded. They were forbidden to leave, on pain of death.
C: The Venetians produced the first really clear glass, and that made two things possible. One was the mirror. The other was the lens. Spectacles appeared in Italy around thirteen hundred, and they added perhaps fifteen years to the working life of every scholar and craftsman. Later, lenses gave us the telescope and the microscope, so it's fair to say that modern science depended on glass.
C: Large flat sheets remained difficult to make until the nineteen fifties, when a British firm developed the float process. Molten glass is poured onto a bath of liquid tin, and it spreads out perfectly flat. Almost every window in the world is now made that way.
C: And the story isn't over. Thin strands of glass, known as optical fibres, now carry nearly all the world's internet traffic under the oceans. A material first used for beads has become the foundation of modern communication.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about wild animals living in cities.
@C Lecturer male_us
C: When we think about wildlife, we tend to picture forests and mountains. But a growing number of species are making their homes in cities, and some are doing better there than in the countryside. Today I want to ask why.
C: Let's start with what cities offer. The first thing is food. Our rubbish is rich in calories and available all year round. The second is warmth. A city centre can be several degrees warmer than the land around it, which helps small animals through the winter. And the third, which people forget, is safety. In a city there are very few hunters, and large predators are almost entirely absent.
C: Not every species can take advantage of this. The successful ones tend to share certain features. They eat a wide range of foods. They're not easily frightened by anything new. And, very often, they're active at night, when the streets are empty.
C: The red fox is the classic example. Foxes began moving into British cities in the nineteen thirties, when suburbs with large gardens were built. A city fox has a much smaller territory than a rural one, because it doesn't need to travel far to eat.
C: What's particularly interesting is that city life seems to change animals. Studies of birds have found that those in noisy areas sing at a higher pitch, so that their song isn't drowned by the low rumble of traffic. Some also sing at night, when it's quieter.
C: There's evidence of change in problem solving too. In one experiment, raccoons from a city and from the country were given a box with a difficult lock. The city animals were faster at opening it, and they kept trying for longer.
C: Evolution can be surprisingly rapid. A species of mosquito in the tunnels of the London Underground can no longer breed with its relatives on the surface. And in several cities, lizards have developed longer legs and stickier feet, which help them to climb walls and windows.
C: But I don't want to give too rosy a picture. Cities are dangerous in their own ways. Roads kill enormous numbers of animals. Glass kills birds, which can't see it. And artificial light confuses insects and migrating birds, with effects we're only beginning to measure.
C: So what should planners do? The most effective step turns out to be connecting green spaces. A park that stands alone is like an island. Link it to others by strips of trees along a river or a railway, and animals can move between them. Such strips are known as corridors, and many cities are now building them into their plans.
