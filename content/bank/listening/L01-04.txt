## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the history of measuring time.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: Today I want to look at how people have measured time, and at how each new method changed the way societies were organised.
C: The earliest devices were shadow clocks, used in Egypt about three and a half thousand years ago. They divided daylight into twelve parts, but the length of each part changed with the season, so a summer hour was longer than a winter one. Nobody found this strange. And of course a shadow clock had one obvious weakness, which is that it could not be used at night.
C: The solution was the water clock, in which water dripped at a steady rate from one container into another. These were used in courts of law in ancient Athens to limit how long each speaker could talk. Their drawback was temperature, because in cold weather the water flowed more slowly and the clock lost time.
C: Mechanical clocks appeared in Europe around the end of the thirteenth century. Interestingly, the first ones had no face and no hands. They simply rang a bell, and the people who needed them most were monks, who had to pray at fixed times of day and night. Early clocks were driven by a falling weight, which meant they had to be placed high up in a tower.
C: The great leap in accuracy came in sixteen fifty-six, when the Dutch scientist Huygens built a clock controlled by a pendulum. Before that, a good clock might lose fifteen minutes a day. Afterwards, the error fell to about fifteen seconds, and for the first time it made sense to add a minute hand.
C: Pendulum clocks were useless at sea, however, because of the movement of the ship. This mattered a great deal, since a navigator needed the exact time in order to work out his longitude. The problem was solved by John Harrison, who was trained not as a scientist but as a carpenter. His fourth timekeeper looked like a large pocket watch, and on a voyage to Jamaica it lost only five seconds.
C: Until the nineteenth century, every town still kept its own local time, set by the sun. Bristol was about ten minutes behind London, and it didn't matter until the arrival of the railways. Timetables were impossible with dozens of local times, so the railway companies imposed a single standard, and the news of the correct time was sent along the lines by telegraph.
C: Finally, in the twentieth century, the pendulum gave way to the vibrations of a quartz crystal, and then to the atomic clock. And it's worth remembering that the satellite navigation in your phone depends entirely on atomic clocks agreeing with one another.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about living things that produce light.
@C Lecturer male_us
C: Good morning. This week we turn to bioluminescence, which simply means light produced by living things. On land it's rare. You may know fireflies and a few kinds of fungus. But in the deep ocean it's the normal state of affairs, and it's estimated that three quarters of the animals living there can make light.
C: Let's start with how it works. The light comes from a chemical reaction. A molecule called luciferin combines with oxygen, and energy is released in the form of light. What's remarkable is the efficiency. An ordinary light bulb wastes most of its energy as heat, whereas this reaction produces almost none, which is why it's sometimes called cold light.
C: The colour is worth noting too. In the sea, nearly all of it is blue, and there's a good reason. Blue light travels furthest through water, while red light is absorbed within a few metres.
C: So what is the light for? Biologists have identified several functions. The first is defence. Some squid, when attacked, release a cloud of glowing liquid, which confuses the predator while the squid escapes into the dark.
C: A second defensive use is harder to spot. Many fish have rows of lights along the belly. Seen from below, the fish would normally appear as a dark shape against the faint light from the surface. By glowing, it hides its own shadow. So here, light is being used as camouflage.
C: Light is also used for hunting. The best known example is the anglerfish, which has a glowing lure hanging in front of its mouth. Interestingly, the fish doesn't make the light itself. The lure is full of bacteria, and these do the work in exchange for food and shelter.
C: The third function is communication. Fireflies are the classic case. Each species has its own pattern of flashes, and males and females use it to find a mate. There's even one species that imitates the signal of another, in order to attract males and eat them.
C: Finally, why should the rest of us care? Well, the genes responsible have become one of the most useful tools in medicine. Researchers attach them to cancer cells, so that a tumour literally glows and its growth can be followed without surgery. The same reaction is used in the food industry, where it shows within minutes whether a surface is contaminated, a test that used to take two days.

Bioluminescence
Background
- uncommon on land, e.g. fireflies and some types of {{11=fungus}}
- produced when luciferin reacts with {{12=oxygen}}
- gives off very little {{13=heat}}
- in the sea, the light is usually {{14=blue}} in colour
Functions
- defence: some squid release a glowing {{15=liquid}}
- lights on a fish's belly act as {{16=camouflage}}
- hunting: the anglerfish's light is made by {{17=bacteria}}
- communication: fireflies flash to find a {{18=mate}}
Uses
- medicine: following the growth of a {{19=tumour}}
- food industry: testing whether a {{20=surface}} is clean
