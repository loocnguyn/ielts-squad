## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the history of maps.
@C Lecturer male_us
C: A map is never a neutral picture of the world. Every map is made by someone, for a purpose, and it leaves out far more than it includes. That's the idea I'd like you to keep in mind as we go through a few episodes in the history of map making.
C: The oldest surviving maps aren't of countries or continents. They're local. A clay tablet from Babylon, about four thousand years old, shows a single estate, with its fields and canals, and it was probably made to settle who owned what.
C: The Greeks took a huge step. They understood that the earth was a sphere, and one of them, Eratosthenes, calculated its size remarkably accurately. He did it by measuring the length of a shadow in two different cities on the same day.
C: In medieval Europe, much of that knowledge was set aside. The great maps of the period were not made for travellers at all. They were made for churches, and their purpose was to teach religion. Jerusalem is placed at the centre, and east, not north, is at the top.
C: Sailors, meanwhile, used something far more practical, the sea chart. These charts, drawn on animal skin, were covered with straight lines radiating from points around the coast. A captain would choose a line and follow it with his compass.
C: In fifteen sixty-nine, Gerard Mercator solved a problem that had troubled navigators for a century. On his map, a course in a constant direction appears as a straight line. The price is distortion. Areas far from the equator are stretched, so Greenland looks as large as Africa, when in reality Africa is fourteen times bigger.
C: Maps have also been tools of power. In the eighteenth century, governments began to map their own territory in detail, and the first aim was usually military. The national mapping agency of Britain was set up because the army feared an invasion from France.
C: Perhaps the most famous map of the twentieth century is the plan of the London Underground, designed in nineteen thirty-three. Its designer, Harry Beck, was not a map maker but an electrical draughtsman, and he drew the system like a circuit diagram. He ignored real distances completely. Passengers, he argued, only needed to know the order of the stations and where to change.
C: Today, of course, the map is in our pockets, and it puts us at the centre, wherever we go. It's worth asking what that does to our sense of direction. Several studies suggest that people who rely on satellite navigation build a poorer mental picture of their surroundings than those who use a paper map.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about how honeybees communicate.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: A honeybee colony may contain fifty thousand individuals, and no one is in charge. The queen lays eggs, but she gives no orders. And yet the colony behaves as if it had a single mind. This morning I'll look at how information moves through the hive.
C: The best known method is the waggle dance. When a bee finds a rich source of food, she returns to the hive and dances on the surface of the comb, in the dark, surrounded by other workers. She runs in a straight line, shaking her body from side to side, then circles back and repeats it.
C: The dance carries two pieces of information. The first is direction. The angle of her straight run, measured from the vertical, matches the angle between the food and the sun. The second is distance, which is shown by how long the waggling lasts. Roughly one second of waggling corresponds to a kilometre of flight.
C: This was worked out in the nineteen forties by an Austrian scientist, Karl von Frisch. He marked individual bees with dots of paint and watched them for years. Many of his colleagues refused to believe that an insect could use a symbolic language, and he was not awarded the Nobel Prize until nineteen seventy-three.
C: Because the hive is dark, the other bees can't see the dance. They follow it by touch, using their antennae, and they also pick up the scent of the flowers on the dancer's body.
C: Dancing isn't the only signal. Chemicals are just as important. The queen produces a substance that is passed from bee to bee as they feed one another. As long as it circulates, the workers know she's alive and well. If she dies, the level drops within hours, and the workers begin raising a new queen.
C: There's also an alarm chemical. When a bee stings, it releases a scent that marks the target and calls other bees to attack the same spot. Curiously, this scent smells of bananas, which is why beekeepers avoid eating them before opening a hive.
C: The most remarkable example of communication comes when a colony needs a new home. Scouts fly out and inspect possible sites, hollow trees for instance. Each returns and dances for the site she has found, and the better the site, the longer she dances. Other scouts go to look, and they in turn dance. Over a day or two, support builds for one site, and when enough scouts agree, the whole swarm takes off together. It's a form of voting, and it almost always selects the best site available.
