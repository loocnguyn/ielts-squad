## 4 | You will hear part of a history lecture about the importance of salt.
@D Lecturer female_us
D: It's hard for us to imagine now, when salt costs almost nothing, but for most of human history it was one of the most valuable substances on earth. In today's lecture I want to explain why, and to show how salt shaped trade, taxation and even language.
D: The reason is simple. Before refrigeration, salt was the main way of preserving food. Meat and fish packed in salt could be kept through the winter or carried on a long voyage. A community without salt could not store food, and a community that could not store food could not survive a bad harvest.
D: Salt was produced in two ways. Near the coast, sea water was run into shallow ponds and left to evaporate in the sun. Inland, it had to be mined. The mine at Hallstatt in Austria was being worked seven thousand years ago, and the miners' leather bags and wooden tools have survived, preserved by the salt itself.
D: Because everyone needed it, salt was easy to tax. In China, a tax on salt was introduced more than two thousand years ago, and at times it provided half of the government's income. In France, a hated salt tax was one of the grievances that led to the revolution.
D: Salt also built roads. In Italy, a very early road, the Via Salaria, was laid out to carry salt from the coast to Rome. And it's often said that Roman soldiers were paid in salt. The evidence for that is actually weak. What we can say is that the word salary comes from the Latin word for salt, probably because soldiers received an allowance to buy it.
D: In West Africa, the salt trade crossed the Sahara. Slabs of salt were cut from the desert floor and carried south by camel, and according to Arab travellers, in some markets salt was exchanged for an equal weight of gold.
D: So what ended salt's importance? Not refrigeration at first, but canning, which was developed in the early nineteenth century to feed armies. Then, with modern drilling, vast underground deposits were discovered, and the price collapsed.
D: Today, less than a tenth of the salt produced is eaten. Most goes to the chemical industry, and in cold countries a huge quantity is spread on roads in winter to melt ice.
D: Ironically, the modern problem is that we eat too much. Doctors link a high intake of salt with raised blood pressure, and most of what we consume is hidden in processed food, especially bread, not added at the table.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about attempts to restore damaged coral reefs.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: Coral reefs cover less than one per cent of the ocean floor, yet they are home to roughly a quarter of all marine species. Over the last thirty years, about half of the world's coral has been lost. Today I'm going to describe some of the methods being used to bring reefs back, and to be honest with you about their limits.
C: First, a reminder of what coral is. It looks like rock or a plant, but it is in fact an animal, or rather a colony of thousands of tiny animals. Inside their tissue live microscopic algae, and it's these that give coral both its colour and most of its food. When the water becomes too warm, the coral expels the algae and turns white. This is known as bleaching, and if it lasts more than a few weeks, the coral starves.
C: The oldest method of restoration is often called coral gardening. Divers break small pieces from a healthy colony and hang them on underwater frames that look rather like trees. After a year or so, the pieces are large enough to be attached to the damaged reef, traditionally with cement, although nowadays a simple clip is often used.
C: A discovery made by accident has speeded this up. A researcher in Florida broke a piece of coral while cleaning a tank and noticed that the fragments grew far faster than the original. Cutting coral into very small pieces, it turns out, makes it grow up to forty times more quickly.
C: A second approach uses sound. A dead reef is silent, and young fish avoid it. In one experiment in Australia, scientists placed speakers on damaged reef and played recordings of a healthy one. The number of fish settling there doubled, and fish matter, because they eat the seaweed that would otherwise smother young coral.
C: A third method works with breeding. Most corals release their eggs on just one or two nights a year, shortly after a full moon. Teams now collect the eggs in fine nets, raise the young in floating pools, and release millions of them over the reef.
C: More controversially, some laboratories are breeding coral selected for its tolerance of heat, in the hope that it will survive in warmer seas.
C: Now for the limits. Restoration is extremely expensive, and so far the largest projects have covered only a few hectares, whereas the Great Barrier Reef alone is the size of Italy. Gardening cannot keep pace with losses on that scale. Most scientists therefore see restoration as a way of buying time, and nothing more, unless the warming of the oceans is slowed.
