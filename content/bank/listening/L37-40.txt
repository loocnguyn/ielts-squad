## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about what ice from Antarctica tells scientists about past climates.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: If you want to know what the earth's climate was like before anyone kept records, one of the best places to look is deep inside the ice of Antarctica. Today I'll explain how ice cores are collected and what we've learned from them.
C: The principle is simple. Snow falls on the ice sheet every year and never melts. It is buried and pressed down by later layers, and over centuries the snow is squeezed into solid ice. So the deeper you go, the older the ice. In the centre of Antarctica, the sheet is more than three kilometres thick.
C: To sample it, scientists use a hollow drill, which brings up cylinders of ice about ten centimetres across. The work can only be done in summer, and even then the temperature may be minus thirty. Drilling to the bottom takes several seasons.
C: What makes the ice so valuable is that it contains tiny bubbles. As the snow is compressed, air is trapped inside it. Those bubbles are samples of the atmosphere from the time the snow fell. By crushing the ice in a vacuum and analysing the air released, we can measure directly how much carbon dioxide the atmosphere held hundreds of thousands of years ago.
C: The ice itself tells us about temperature. Water molecules come in slightly lighter and heavier forms, and the proportion in snow depends on how cold it was when the snow formed. So one core gives two records, temperature and carbon dioxide, side by side.
C: There are other clues. Layers of ash mark volcanic eruptions, and since many eruptions have known dates, they help us to check the age of the ice. Dust shows how windy and dry the world was. During ice ages, the ice contains far more dust.
C: The longest continuous record so far comes from a site called Dome C, and it reaches back eight hundred thousand years. It shows eight ice ages, each lasting about a hundred thousand years, separated by shorter warm periods.
C: And here's the central finding. Through all those cycles, temperature and carbon dioxide rose and fell together. In that entire record, carbon dioxide never rose above three hundred parts per million. Today it's over four hundred and twenty, and the speed of the increase is at least a hundred times faster than anything in the ice.
C: A new project is now searching for ice one and a half million years old. The reason is a puzzle. Before about a million years ago, ice ages came every forty thousand years, not every hundred thousand, and nobody knows why the rhythm changed.

## 2 | You will hear a speaker on a radio programme giving advice about wasting less food at home.
@C Speaker female_us
C: Thanks, Mike. Yes, today I'm talking about food waste. The average household in this country throws away around seventy kilos of edible food a year. That's roughly sixty pounds a month for a family of four, straight into the bin.
C: When I ask people what they waste most, they say meat or dairy. But the surveys are clear. The food we throw away in the largest quantity is bread, followed by potatoes and then salad.
C: So what can you do? My first tip is the most boring and the most effective. Before you go shopping, look in the fridge and write a list. People who shop with a list waste about a quarter less than people who don't.
C: Second, understand the dates on the packet. A use-by date is about safety. You shouldn't eat the food after it. A best-before date is only about quality. Biscuits a month past their best-before date may be a little soft, but they won't hurt you. Half of us, according to one study, treat the two as the same, and throw away perfectly good food.
C: Third, check the temperature of your fridge. It should be below five degrees. Most domestic fridges are set too warm, at around seven, and at that temperature milk lasts two days less.
C: Where you keep things matters as well. Tomatoes and bananas don't belong in the fridge. The cold spoils the flavour. Potatoes should be stored somewhere dark, and kept apart from onions, because each makes the other go off faster.
C: Fourth, love your freezer. Nearly everything can be frozen. Bread, of course. Slice it first, and you can toast it straight from frozen. But also grated cheese, milk, and even eggs, as long as you crack them out of their shells and beat them first.
C: And fifth, use what's left over. My rule is that Thursday is use-it-up night in our house. Whatever vegetables remain go into a soup or an omelette. My children complain, and then they eat it.
C: A word about technology. There are now apps that let cafes and supermarkets sell unsold food cheaply at the end of the day. You pay about a third of the normal price and collect a bag in the evening. You don't know what's in it until you open it, which is half the fun.
C: Finally, whatever can't be eaten, peelings and tea bags and so on, should go in a compost bin, not the general rubbish. Buried in landfill, food rots and produces methane, which is a far more powerful greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about what economists have learned about happiness.
@D Lecturer male_us
D: For most of its history, economics assumed that people want more income, and that more income makes them better off. That seems obvious. But about fifty years ago, an economist named Richard Easterlin looked at the data and found something odd.
D: Within any one country, at any one time, richer people do report being happier than poorer people. No surprise there. But when Easterlin followed countries over decades, he found that as a nation grew richer, the average level of happiness hardly moved. The United States tripled its income per head after the war, and Americans were no more satisfied than before. This became known as the Easterlin paradox.
D: How can both things be true? There are two main explanations. The first is comparison. We judge our income not in absolute terms but against the people around us. In one survey, students were asked to choose between two worlds. In the first, they earn fifty thousand dollars and others earn twenty-five. In the second, they earn a hundred thousand, and others earn two hundred. A majority preferred the first.
D: The second explanation is adaptation. We get used to things. A larger house is a delight for about a year, and then it's simply the house. Studies of lottery winners find that after the first excitement, most return to roughly their earlier level of happiness.
D: Interestingly, there are things we don't adapt to. One is a long journey to work. People consistently underestimate how much it will cost them in well-being. Another is noise. And another, on the positive side, is time with friends, which goes on giving pleasure however often it's repeated.
D: So what does predict happiness, if not ever more income? The largest long-term study, which has followed a group of men from Harvard since nineteen thirty-eight, gives a clear answer. The best predictor of a happy and healthy old age was not wealth, or fame, or even cholesterol. It was the quality of a person's relationships.
D: Unemployment, by contrast, is devastating, and far more so than the loss of income alone would explain. People value work for the purpose and status it provides.
D: What follows for policy? Some governments now measure well-being directly, alongside national income. And some economists argue that spending on mental health would raise national happiness more cheaply than almost anything else.
D: A final caution. Critics point out that asking people to rate their lives from one to ten is a crude measure, and that the answers are influenced by small things, even by the weather on the day of the survey.
