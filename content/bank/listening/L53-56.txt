## 4 | You will hear part of an architecture lecture about the development of the skyscraper.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: The skyscraper is so much a part of the modern city that it's easy to forget how recently it became possible. Until the eighteen eighties, the tallest ordinary buildings had five or six storeys. Today I'll explain the three inventions that changed that, and what limits remain.
C: The first obstacle was the wall. In a traditional building, the walls carry the weight of everything above them. The higher you build, the thicker the walls at the bottom must be. The Monadnock Building in Chicago, sixteen storeys of brick, has walls nearly two metres thick at the base, and they take up a great deal of the ground floor.
C: The solution was the steel frame. In a framed building, a skeleton of steel columns and beams carries the load, and the walls simply hang on it, like a curtain. The first building of this kind went up in Chicago in eighteen eighty-five. It had only ten storeys, but the principle was established.
C: The second obstacle was human. Nobody will climb twenty flights of stairs. Lifts existed, but people didn't trust them, because the ropes sometimes broke. The breakthrough came from Elisha Otis. At an exhibition in New York in eighteen fifty-four, he stood on a platform high above the crowd and ordered the rope to be cut. His safety brake held. Interestingly, the lift reversed the pattern of rents. Before it, the cheapest rooms were at the top. Afterwards, the top floors became the most expensive.
C: The third requirement is less obvious. A tall building full of people needs fresh air, and above a certain height you can't simply open a window, because of the wind. So the skyscraper depended on mechanical ventilation, and later on air conditioning.
C: With those three things in place, buildings rose quickly. But a new enemy appeared, and that was the wind. A very tall tower sways, and although it's perfectly safe, the people on the upper floors feel sick. Engineers now fit a huge weight near the top, which swings in the opposite direction and cancels the movement. The one in Taipei weighs six hundred and sixty tonnes, and it's become a tourist attraction.
C: So what limits height today? Not structure. Engineers could build far higher. The real limit is the lift. The taller the tower, the more lift shafts it needs, and eventually they occupy so much of each floor that there's too little space left to rent.
C: A final point. Tall buildings are sometimes defended as green, because they use little land. But they need a great deal of energy to build and run, and several studies suggest that buildings of six to ten storeys are more efficient.

## 2 | You will hear a gardening expert on the radio giving advice about growing vegetables on a balcony.
@C Expert male_us
C: Thanks, Julie. Yes, this week's letter comes from a listener who lives in a flat on the fourth floor and wants to know whether it's worth trying to grow food on a balcony. My answer is a definite yes, as long as you avoid a few common mistakes.
C: The first thing to check is weight. Wet soil is heavy. A large pot full of compost can weigh as much as an adult, and a balcony has its limits. So before you buy anything, ask the owner of the building how much load the balcony can take. Use plastic or fabric pots, not stone ones, and place the heaviest containers close to the wall, where the structure is strongest.
C: Second, light. Most vegetables need at least six hours of direct sun. If your balcony faces north, don't despair. You can still grow salad leaves, spinach and herbs such as mint and parsley, which are happy in shade.
C: The third factor, and it's the one people forget, is wind. On a high balcony, wind dries out plants far faster than sun does, and it can snap tall stems. I'd put up a screen of netting, which filters the wind without blocking the light.
C: Now, what to grow. Beginners always want tomatoes, and they do well, provided you choose a small bush variety and not one that climbs to two metres. But if you want my top recommendation, it's salad leaves. They're ready in four weeks, and you cut what you need and they grow again.
C: Chillies are another good choice. They love the heat reflected from a wall. And don't overlook potatoes. You can grow them in a strong bag, adding compost as the shoots appear, and children love tipping out the bag at the end to find the crop.
C: What I wouldn't bother with is carrots, which need deep soil, or sweetcorn, which has to be planted in a large block if it's to produce anything.
C: The biggest cause of failure is watering. Pots dry out quickly, and in summer they may need water twice a day. If you're out at work, stand each pot in a deep saucer, or fit a simple drip system with a timer. They cost about twenty pounds.
C: Feed the plants once a week from midsummer. There's very little goodness in a pot of compost, and it's used up within six weeks.
C: Finally, think about your neighbours. Water running off your balcony onto the one below is the fastest way to lose friends. Saucers under every pot, please.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about mangrove forests.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: If you stand on a tropical coast where a river meets the sea, you may find yourself facing a wall of tangled roots rising out of the mud. This is a mangrove forest, and although it isn't beautiful in the usual sense, it's among the most valuable ecosystems on earth.
C: Mangroves are trees that have solved a problem that kills almost every other plant, which is salt water. Different species manage this in different ways. Some filter out the salt at the roots. Others take it in and get rid of it through their leaves, and if you lick a leaf, you can taste it.
C: The second problem is the mud itself, which contains almost no oxygen. So many mangroves send up roots that stick out of the mud like pencils and take in air directly. Others stand on arching roots that look like stilts.
C: Their seeds are unusual as well. In most species, the seed begins to grow while it's still attached to the parent tree. When it drops, it's already a young plant, shaped like a spear. If the tide is out, it sticks in the mud. If the tide is in, it floats, sometimes for months, until it reaches a shore.
C: Now, why do these forests matter? First, as a nursery. The roots give shelter to young fish and shrimps. It's estimated that three quarters of the fish caught commercially in the tropics spend part of their lives among mangroves.
C: Second, as protection. The roots break the force of the waves. After the tsunami of two thousand and four, studies in India and Sri Lanka found that villages behind intact mangroves suffered far fewer deaths than those where the trees had been cleared.
C: Third, and this has only been appreciated recently, mangroves store carbon. The mud beneath them holds up to four times as much per hectare as a tropical rainforest, because dead material doesn't rot in mud without oxygen.
C: Despite all this, we've lost about a third of the world's mangroves in the last fifty years. The greatest single cause has been the farming of shrimps. The trees are cut down to make ponds, and the ponds are often abandoned after five years, when disease sets in.
C: Can the forests be restored? Early attempts frequently failed. People planted thousands of young trees in neat rows, often of a single species, in places that were flooded too deeply. Most of them died. The more successful approach is to repair the natural flow of water, by opening up blocked channels, and then to let the seeds arrive by themselves on the tide.
