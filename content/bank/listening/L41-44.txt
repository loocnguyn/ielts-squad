## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the early history of vaccination.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: Smallpox killed perhaps three hundred million people in the twentieth century alone, and today it no longer exists outside two laboratories. That is the greatest achievement in the history of medicine, and this afternoon I'd like to trace how it began.
C: People had noticed long ago that anyone who survived smallpox never caught it again. From that observation came a practice known as inoculation. Material from the sore of a patient with a mild case was scratched into the skin of a healthy person. The person usually developed a mild illness and was protected for life. The method was used in China and India, and in parts of Africa, centuries before it reached Europe.
C: It arrived in England in seventeen twenty-one, thanks to Lady Mary Wortley Montagu. She was the wife of the British ambassador to Turkey, she had been scarred by smallpox herself, and she'd seen the procedure carried out in Constantinople. She had her own children inoculated, and persuaded the royal family to do the same.
C: But inoculation was risky. Roughly one person in fifty died of it, and those who'd been treated could pass on the real disease to others.
C: The safer alternative came from the countryside. It was common knowledge among farmers that milkmaids rarely caught smallpox. They did catch cowpox, a mild disease of cattle that produced a few sores on the hands.
C: In seventeen ninety-six, a country doctor named Edward Jenner put this to the test. He took material from a cowpox sore on the hand of a milkmaid and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy, the son of his gardener. Six weeks later, he deliberately tried to infect the boy with smallpox. Nothing happened. By modern standards, the experiment was deeply unethical, but it worked.
C: Jenner called his method vaccination, from the Latin word for cow. It spread with astonishing speed. Within five years, it was in use from Russia to the United States.
C: There was opposition too. Cartoons showed patients growing horns. And when the British government made vaccination compulsory for babies in eighteen fifty-three, there were riots, and an organised movement against it.
C: One difficulty was transport. The material didn't keep, and there was no refrigeration. So when Spain sent vaccine to its colonies in eighteen hundred and three, it did so in the arms of twenty-two orphan boys. Two were vaccinated before sailing, and the infection was passed from one pair to the next throughout the voyage.
C: The final chapter came in nineteen eighty, when the World Health Organization declared that smallpox had been wiped out.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about how animals find their way over long distances.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: Each autumn, a small bird called the Arctic tern leaves Greenland and flies to Antarctica, and each spring it flies back. Over a lifetime of thirty years, that's equal to three return trips to the moon. How does it know the way? Today I'll outline what we understand about animal navigation, and what remains a mystery.
C: Navigation requires two things. You need a compass, to tell you which direction is which. And you need a map, to tell you where you are in relation to your goal. A compass by itself isn't enough. If I blindfolded you, drove you a hundred kilometres and gave you a compass, you'd know where north was, but not which way was home.
C: Let's take compasses first, since they're better understood. Many birds use the sun. That's harder than it sounds, because the sun moves across the sky, so the bird must allow for the time of day. Experiments in which birds' internal clocks were shifted by six hours showed that they then flew off at ninety degrees to the correct course.
C: Birds that migrate at night use the stars. Young birds raised in a planetarium learned to identify north from the point around which the stars rotate. They weren't born knowing a star map. They learned it by watching the sky turn.
C: The third compass is the earth's magnetic field. This was proved in the nineteen sixties with robins kept in a cage. When researchers used electric coils to turn the magnetic field around the cage, the birds changed direction to match. We still aren't certain how they detect it. The leading theory involves a chemical reaction in the eye, which would mean that birds, in some sense, see the field.
C: Now the map, which is far more puzzling. Sea turtles hatch on a beach, spend twenty years wandering the ocean, and return to lay eggs on the very beach where they were born. The evidence suggests that each stretch of coast has its own magnetic signature, and that the young turtle memorises it.
C: Salmon do something similar at sea, but for the last stage, finding the right river, they rely on smell. Each stream has a particular chemical character, and the fish follow it home.
C: And insects shouldn't be overlooked. Desert ants find their way back to the nest in a straight line after a winding search for food. They do it by counting their steps. When researchers attached tiny stilts to their legs, the ants walked straight past the nest.
C: A final thought. Artificial light at night and electrical noise from our cities both interfere with these senses, and we're only beginning to measure the damage.
