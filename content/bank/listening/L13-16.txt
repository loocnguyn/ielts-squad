## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about how writing was invented.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: Writing seems so natural to us that it's easy to forget it had to be invented. In fact, as far as we know, it was invented independently only a handful of times, in Mesopotamia, in China, and in Central America. Today I'll concentrate on the earliest of these, in what is now Iraq, a little over five thousand years ago.
C: The first surprise is what writing was for. We might imagine that people wanted to record stories, or prayers, or the deeds of kings. Not at all. The earliest tablets are lists of goods. Writing began as a tool for accounting.
C: The story really starts earlier, with small clay tokens. For thousands of years before writing, farmers in the region used these to keep track of what they owned. A token of one shape stood for a sheep, another for a jar of oil. To record a debt of five sheep, you handed over five tokens.
C: The difficulty was trust. Tokens could be lost or stolen. So people began sealing them inside a hollow ball of clay, a kind of envelope. That created a new problem, because you couldn't see what was inside without breaking it. The answer was to press the tokens into the wet surface of the envelope before sealing it, leaving marks that showed the contents.
C: And then somebody realised that the tokens themselves were no longer necessary. The marks were enough. The envelope was flattened into a tablet, and the marks were drawn with a cut reed. Because the reed left a shape like a wedge, we call this script cuneiform, from the Latin for wedge.
C: At this stage, the signs were pictures. The great step forward was to use a sign not for its meaning but for its sound. It's as if we drew a bee and a leaf to write the word belief. Once that happened, names could be written, and then whole sentences of the spoken language.
C: Learning the system was hard. There were about six hundred signs, and a boy training to be a scribe spent some twelve years at school. We have their exercise tablets, with the teacher's neat version on one side and the pupil's clumsy copy on the other.
C: Because clay survives fire, when other materials are destroyed, hundreds of thousands of tablets have come down to us. Most are receipts and contracts. But among them are letters, and I'll end with my favourite. It's from a merchant complaining that the copper he was sold was of poor quality, and demanding his money back. It is the oldest known letter of complaint.

C: Next, the material passes over a series of rotating discs. Cardboard, being large and flat, rides over the top, while smaller objects fall through.
C: After that, a powerful magnet lifts out anything made of steel, mainly food tins. Aluminium isn't magnetic, so drinks cans are removed further along by a different machine.
C: Plastics are the cleverest part. The bottles pass under an infrared light, which identifies the type of plastic, and a jet of air blows each one into the correct container. It happens faster than you can see.
C: Glass is the last thing left on the belt. It's broken into small pieces and sent to a factory forty miles away, where it becomes new bottles.
C: At the end, everything is pressed into bales and sold. I'm sometimes asked whether it all ends up abroad. Paper does go overseas, but our plastic and metal are processed in this country.
C: Right. Ear protection on, please, and follow me.

## 4 | You will hear part of a botany lecture about how plants survive in deserts.
@C Lecturer male_us
C: Deserts are defined by a shortage of water, and yet they contain several thousand species of plant. This morning I want to look at the strategies those plants use, and I'll group them under three headings, which are escaping drought, resisting it, and tolerating it.
C: Let's begin with the escapers. These are annual plants that avoid drought altogether by living very short lives. Their seeds lie in the soil, sometimes for decades. When heavy rain finally arrives, they germinate, flower and produce new seeds within a few weeks. What's clever is that the seeds are coated with a chemical that prevents growth, and only a really heavy fall of rain will wash it off. A light shower, which wouldn't keep a plant alive, doesn't trigger them.
C: Second, the resisters, and the best known are the cacti. A cactus stores water in its stem, and that stem is folded, rather like an accordion, so that it can expand after rain. A large cactus can take up two hundred litres in a single storm.
C: To avoid losing that water, a cactus has no leaves. What it has are spines. These protect it from thirsty animals, of course, but they also cast a surprising amount of shade on the surface of the plant.
C: Most plants open the pores in their surface during the day, to take in carbon dioxide. A cactus does the opposite. It opens its pores only at night, when the air is cooler, and stores the gas until the sun comes up.
C: Roots matter too. People imagine a desert plant sending a root deep underground, and some trees do exactly that. One, the mesquite, has been found with roots fifty metres down. But a cactus spreads its roots wide and very close to the surface, to catch rain before it evaporates.
C: Third, the tolerators, which I find the most remarkable. These plants can lose almost all their water and survive. One small fern, known as the resurrection plant, curls into a dry brown ball and appears completely dead. Given water, it turns green again within a day. It protects its cells by replacing the water with a kind of sugar.
C: Why does any of this matter beyond botany? Well, as the climate warms, more farmland is becoming dry. Researchers are trying to transfer some of these abilities to crops. Much of the work is being done on rice, with wheat likely to follow. If they succeed, the humble plants of the desert may turn out to be among the most valuable on earth.
