## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the placebo effect in medicine.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: This afternoon's topic is one of the strangest in medicine. A placebo is a treatment with no active ingredient, a sugar pill, for instance, and yet patients who take one very often improve. I want to look at what we know about this, and at what it does and doesn't mean.
C: The modern study of the subject is usually traced to the Second World War. An American army doctor, Henry Beecher, ran short of morphine. A nurse, according to the story, injected a wounded soldier with salt water and told him it was a powerful painkiller. To Beecher's amazement, the soldier's pain eased.
C: After the war, Beecher argued that every new drug should be tested against a placebo, and that's now the standard. In a proper trial, neither the patients nor the doctors know who is receiving the real drug. We call that a double-blind trial.
C: What have such trials taught us about placebos themselves? First, the form of the treatment matters. Two pills work better than one. An injection works better than a pill. Even the colour has an influence. In several studies, blue pills were better at helping people sleep, while red ones were more effective as stimulants.
C: Second, the price matters. In one experiment, volunteers were given the same dummy painkiller. Half were told it cost two and a half dollars a pill, and half were told it cost ten cents. The supposedly expensive pill relieved pain much more effectively.
C: Third, the relationship with the doctor matters. Patients who are given time, and who feel that they've been listened to, improve more than those who are hurried through.
C: Now, an important caution. Placebos change how patients feel. They affect symptoms such as pain, nausea and tiredness. They don't shrink tumours or mend broken bones. Asthma patients given a dummy inhaler reported feeling much better, but when their breathing was measured, it hadn't improved at all.
C: Is a placebo simply imagination? Not entirely. Brain scans show that when people expect relief from pain, the brain releases its own natural painkillers. So the effect is real, in the sense that it has a physical basis.
C: The most surprising recent finding concerns honesty. Researchers at Harvard gave patients pills in a bottle with a label that clearly said placebo, and explained that they contained no medicine. Those patients still did better than a group who received no treatment.
C: Finally, there's a darker side, known as the nocebo effect. If patients are warned that a pill may cause headaches, a good number will develop headaches, even when the pill is made of sugar. That raises a real dilemma about how much doctors should say about side effects.

## 4 | You will hear part of an engineering lecture about the history of concrete.
@C Lecturer female_us
C: Concrete is the most widely used man-made material on the planet. We pour enough of it each year to build a wall around the equator. Yet most people know almost nothing about it, so today I'll sketch its history and then look at the problem it now poses.
C: First, a definition, because people confuse concrete with cement. Cement is a grey powder, and it's only one ingredient. Mix it with water, sand and small stones, and you get concrete. The cement is the glue that binds the rest.
C: The Romans were the first great builders in concrete. Their recipe used lime and a volcanic ash found near Naples. This produced a material that would even set under water, which let them build harbours. Their most famous concrete structure is the Pantheon in Rome. Its dome, almost two thousand years old, is still the largest in the world made of concrete without steel inside it.
C: How did they keep such a dome from collapsing under its own weight? By varying the mix. At the base, the concrete contains heavy stone. Near the top, they used pumice, a volcanic rock so light that it floats.
C: When the Roman empire fell, the recipe was lost for over a thousand years. Modern concrete dates from eighteen twenty-four, when an English bricklayer, Joseph Aspdin, patented what he called Portland cement, because its colour reminded him of a stone quarried at Portland on the south coast.
C: Concrete has one great weakness. It's enormously strong when you squeeze it, but it cracks easily when it's stretched or bent. The solution, discovered in France, came from an unlikely source. A gardener named Joseph Monier was tired of his clay flowerpots breaking, and in the eighteen sixties he made pots of concrete with a mesh of iron wire inside. The idea of reinforced concrete was born, and it made tall buildings and long bridges possible.
C: Ironically, the steel is also the reason modern concrete doesn't last as long as Roman concrete. Water slowly gets in, the steel rusts, and as it rusts it expands and breaks the concrete from within.
C: And there's a larger problem. Making cement means heating limestone to about fourteen hundred degrees, and the process releases carbon dioxide. Cement production is responsible for roughly eight per cent of the world's emissions, more than aviation.
C: So researchers are looking for alternatives. Some replace part of the cement with ash, a waste product from power stations. Others are developing concrete that absorbs carbon dioxide as it hardens. And one team has gone back to the Romans, whose concrete, we've learned, could heal its own cracks.
