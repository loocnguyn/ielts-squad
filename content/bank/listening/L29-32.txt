## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about the science of taste.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: Most of us learned at school that the tongue has a map, with sweetness tasted at the tip and bitterness at the back. It's a neat picture, and it's wrong. It came from the mistranslation of a German paper published in nineteen hundred and one. In reality, every part of the tongue can detect every taste. Today I'll go through what science now says.
C: There are five basic tastes that everyone accepts. Sweet, sour, salty and bitter you know. The fifth is umami, a Japanese word meaning roughly a pleasant savoury taste. It was identified in nineteen hundred and eight by a chemist in Tokyo, who wondered why a soup made from seaweed was so satisfying.
C: Each taste seems to have a purpose. Sweetness signals energy. Saltiness signals minerals the body needs. Umami indicates protein. Sourness can warn that fruit isn't ripe, or that food has gone off. And bitterness is above all a warning of poison, since many toxic plants are bitter. That's why young children refuse bitter vegetables. It's an instinct, and they generally grow out of it.
C: People differ a great deal. About a quarter of the population are what researchers call supertasters. They have far more taste buds than average, and they experience bitterness, in particular, much more intensely. They often dislike coffee and strong cheese.
C: But here's the essential point. Most of what we call taste isn't taste at all. It's smell. When you chew, air carries the scent of the food up the back of the throat to the nose. Try holding your nose while you eat a piece of apple and a piece of raw onion. You'll find them surprisingly hard to tell apart.
C: The other senses join in too. In a well-known experiment, wine experts were given a white wine that had been coloured red. They described it in the language normally used for red wine. Sound matters as well. People judge crisps to be fresher when the crunch they hear is made louder.
C: Even the plate has an effect. A dessert served on a white plate is rated sweeter than the same dessert on a black one. And heavy cutlery makes people think a meal is of higher quality and worth paying more for.
C: Airlines have learned a practical lesson from all this. In a cabin, the dry air and the constant noise of the engines dull our sense of sweetness and saltiness by up to thirty per cent, which is why airline food is so heavily seasoned. Umami, curiously, isn't affected, and that may explain why tomato juice is so popular on planes.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about how scientists monitor volcanoes.
@D Lecturer female_us
D: About eight hundred million people live within a hundred kilometres of an active volcano. We can't prevent eruptions, but we're increasingly able to see them coming. Today I'll describe the main techniques, and I'll finish with a case where they saved many lives.
D: The oldest and still the most important tool is the seismometer, which records vibrations in the ground. Before an eruption, molten rock, or magma, forces its way upward, cracking the rock around it, and this produces swarms of small earthquakes. What scientists watch for is not their strength but their depth. If the earthquakes become shallower day by day, the magma is rising.
D: The second sign is a change in shape. As magma collects beneath it, a volcano swells, rather like a balloon. The movement may be only a few centimetres, far too small to see, but it can be measured from satellites using radar.
D: Third, gas. Magma contains dissolved gases, and they escape as it nears the surface. A sudden rise in sulphur dioxide is one of the most reliable warnings. Collecting samples by hand at the crater is dangerous, so nowadays the job is often done by drones.
D: Fourth, temperature. Cameras that detect heat can show ground warming up weeks before anything is visible. Lakes inside craters are monitored too. If the water changes colour or becomes more acidic, something is happening below.
D: Now, none of these signs is enough alone. Many volcanoes swell and shake and then go quiet again. The skill lies in combining the evidence, and in knowing the history of the particular mountain.
D: Let me turn to my example. In nineteen ninety-one, Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines had been silent for five hundred years. In April, small explosions began. A team installed instruments within days. Crucially, they also studied the deposits left by ancient eruptions, and realised that when this volcano erupted, it erupted on an enormous scale.
D: As the earthquakes multiplied, the alert level was raised step by step, and some sixty thousand people were moved. The eruption, on the fifteenth of June, was the second largest of the century. Several hundred people died, mostly when roofs collapsed under wet ash. Without the evacuation, the figure would have been in the tens of thousands.
D: The hardest part of the job, though, isn't the science. It's communication. A warning that proves to be a false alarm costs money and destroys trust, so that people may refuse to leave the next time. Volcanologists must give clear advice while being honest about uncertainty, and that's a skill no instrument can provide.
