## 2 | You will hear a fire officer giving a talk to residents about preventing fires at home.
@C Officer female_uk
C: Good evening. I'm Station Officer Kate Rowe, and I've been asked to talk for a few minutes about keeping your home safe from fire.
C: Let me start with a question. Where do most house fires begin? People usually say the living room, because of candles and open fires. In fact, more than half start in the kitchen, and the most common cause is simply leaving a pan on the heat and walking away. So my first piece of advice costs nothing. If you're called away, to the door or the phone, turn the cooker off first.
C: If a pan of oil does catch fire, never throw water on it. The water turns to steam in an instant and throws burning oil across the room. Turn off the heat if you can reach the control safely, leave the kitchen, close the door, and call us.
C: The second great cause is electrical. I'm often asked about old wiring. It can be a problem, but far more fires are started by overloaded sockets, where four or five appliances run from a single point. Heaters and kettles should always have a wall socket to themselves.
C: A newer risk is the charger. Batteries for phones, and especially for electric bikes and scooters, can overheat. Please don't charge them overnight, and never in the hallway, because if they catch fire, they block your way out.
C: Now, smoke alarms. You need at least one on every floor. The best place is the ceiling of the hall or landing, not the kitchen, where steam and toast will set it off and tempt you to remove the battery. Test each alarm once a month by pressing the button, and replace the whole unit after ten years.
C: Every household should also have an escape plan. Decide on your route, and make sure everyone knows where the door keys are kept. At night, close the doors of the rooms downstairs. A closed door can hold back a fire for twenty minutes.
C: And if the worst happens, get out, stay out, and call the fire service. Don't go back for pets or possessions. More people are killed by smoke than by flames, and most of them are trying to rescue something.
C: Finally, my service offers a free home safety visit. An officer will come to your house, check your alarms, and fit new ones if needed, at no charge. Priority goes to people over sixty-five and families with young children. You can book through the number on the leaflet.

## 4 | You will hear part of a psychology lecture about memory.
@C Lecturer male_us
C: We tend to imagine memory as a kind of recording, a film that we can play back. Almost everything psychologists have learned in the last century suggests that this picture is false. Memory isn't a recording. It's a reconstruction, built afresh each time we remember.
C: Let me begin with the basic structure. Information first enters short-term memory, which is very limited. It holds about seven items, and for only about twenty seconds unless we keep repeating them. To last, a memory must be transferred to long-term storage, and that process depends heavily on sleep. Students who stay up all night before an exam are working against their own brains.
C: What decides whether something is transferred? Above all, meaning. In a classic experiment, chess masters were shown a board for five seconds and could reproduce it almost perfectly. But when the pieces were placed at random, in positions that could never occur in a game, the masters did no better than beginners. They weren't remembering pieces. They were remembering patterns that meant something to them.
C: Emotion matters too. Events that frighten or delight us are remembered vividly. But vivid doesn't mean accurate. The day after the space shuttle Challenger exploded, a psychologist asked his students to write down how they'd heard the news. Three years later, he asked them again. A quarter gave a completely different account, and they were astonished when they were shown their own handwriting.
C: This brings me to the work of Elizabeth Loftus. She showed people a film of a car accident, and then asked how fast the cars were going when they hit each other. With other viewers, she changed one word, and asked how fast they were going when they smashed into each other. The second group gave higher speeds, and a week later, many of them remembered seeing broken glass, although there was none in the film.
C: The implications for courts of law are serious. Juries find a confident witness very convincing. Yet research shows that confidence and accuracy are only weakly related.
C: So is forgetting simply a failure? Not at all. A brain that kept everything would be overwhelmed by detail. Forgetting lets us keep the general lesson and discard the rest.
C: Finally, some practical advice, since you all have exams. Rereading your notes feels productive, but it's one of the least effective methods. Far better is to close the book and test yourself. And spread your study over several days. An hour on each of three days beats three hours in one evening.

## 4 | You will hear part of a lecture about soil.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: We walk on it, we build on it, and we rarely give it a thought. Yet soil is one of the most complex materials on the planet, and almost all of our food depends on it. This morning I'd like to introduce what soil is and why scientists are worried about it.
C: Soil has four ingredients. There are particles of rock, broken down over thousands of years. There's organic matter, which is the remains of dead plants and animals. And there's air and water, held in the spaces between. A healthy soil is roughly half solid and half space.
C: The rock particles come in three sizes. The largest is sand, the smallest is clay, and in between is silt. The proportions decide how a soil behaves. A sandy soil drains quickly and warms up early in spring, but it holds few nutrients. Clay is rich, but heavy and slow to drain. The ideal for most crops is a mixture that gardeners call loam.
C: What really distinguishes soil from crushed rock, though, is life. A teaspoon of healthy soil contains more living organisms than there are people on earth. The majority are bacteria and fungi. Fungi form fine threads that connect to the roots of plants. The plant supplies the fungus with sugar, and in return the fungus brings water and minerals from much further away than the roots could reach.
C: Then there are the larger creatures, and the earthworm is the most important. Its tunnels let air and water into the ground. Charles Darwin spent forty years studying earthworms. In his last book, he suggested that no other animal had played so important a part in the history of the world.
C: Soil forms extremely slowly. It can take five hundred years to produce two centimetres. And that's why erosion is so serious. When land is ploughed and left bare, wind and rain can remove in one season what took centuries to form. The classic example is the Dust Bowl of the nineteen thirties in the United States, when drought struck land that had been ploughed too deeply, and storms carried the soil as far as the Atlantic.
C: Farmers have responded. Many now leave the remains of the previous crop on the surface, and sow the next one without ploughing. Others plant what's called a cover crop in the winter, purely to protect the ground.
C: Finally, soil matters for the climate. It holds about three times as much carbon as the atmosphere. Manage it badly and that carbon escapes. Manage it well and soil could absorb a significant share of our emissions.
