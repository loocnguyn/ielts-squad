@C Coach male_uk
C: Evening, everybody. It's great to see so many of you. I count twenty-three, which is the biggest group we've ever started with. My name's Pete, and I'll be your coach for the next nine weeks.
C: Let me explain the idea. The programme takes people who do no running at all and gets them to the point where they can run for thirty minutes without stopping. We don't talk about distance at this stage, only time. For most people, half an hour works out at something between four and five kilometres.
C: We do it gradually. Tonight, you'll run for one minute and walk for a minute and a half, and repeat that eight times. That's all. I promise you'll go home thinking it was too easy. That's deliberate. The people who get injured are nearly always the ones who do too much in the first fortnight.

C: A word about speed. Please forget about it. The right pace is one at which you could hold a conversation. If you can't speak a full sentence, you're going too fast. I'd honestly prefer to see you jog slower than you walk than see you sprint and stop.
C: Now, what do you need? Not much. Comfortable clothes, and a proper pair of running shoes. They don't have to be expensive. What matters is that they fit, with a thumb's width of space in front of your toes, and that they aren't worn out. C: As the evenings get darker, wear something bright, or a reflective band.

C: Eat something light about two hours before you run, not immediately before. And drink water during the day, so that you don't need to carry a bottle for a session as short as these.
C: In week nine, we all run together in the Saturday morning event here in the park. It's free, it's five kilometres, and nobody comes last, because a volunteer always walks at the back.

## 4 | You will hear part of a history lecture about the potato.
@C Lecturer female_uk
C: It would be hard to find a more ordinary food than the potato. Yet its arrival in Europe changed the continent's history, and this afternoon I'll explain how.
C: The potato was first grown about eight thousand years ago, high in the Andes, in what is now Peru and Bolivia. The farmers there developed thousands of varieties, and they invented a way of preserving them. They left the potatoes out to freeze at night, and trampled them by day to squeeze out the water. The result could be stored for ten years.
C: Spanish sailors brought the plant to Europe in about fifteen seventy. They carried it not as a treasure but simply as cheap food for the voyage home. And for nearly two centuries, most Europeans refused to eat it. There were several reasons. It wasn't mentioned in the Bible. It grew underground, which seemed unnatural. And it belongs to the same family as some poisonous plants, so people believed it caused disease.
C: Governments, however, saw its value. An acre of potatoes fed three or four times as many people as an acre of wheat. And potatoes had a military advantage. An army marching through could seize or burn a field of grain, but potatoes stayed hidden in the ground.
C: So rulers tried persuasion. In Prussia, the king ordered peasants to plant them. In France, a scientist named Parmentier used a cleverer method. He planted a field near Paris and had it guarded by soldiers during the day. At night, the guards were withdrawn. Local people, assuming that anything guarded must be valuable, crept in and stole the plants.
C: By eighteen hundred, the potato had been accepted, and its effect was dramatic. The population of Europe roughly doubled in a century, and many historians give the potato much of the credit. It fed the workers who moved to the new industrial cities.
C: But there was a hidden danger. Unlike the farmers of the Andes, Europeans grew only a few varieties. In Ireland, a third of the population depended on a single type. When a disease called blight arrived from America in eighteen forty-five, it destroyed the crop three years out of four. About a million people died, and a million more left the country.
C: The lesson has not been forgotten. Scientists now keep collections of wild and traditional potatoes, so that their genes can be used to breed resistance. The largest is in Peru, where the story began. It holds more than four thousand varieties.

## 4 | You will hear part of a biology lecture about the microbes that live in the human body.
@C Lecturer male_uk
C: You probably think of yourself as an individual. A biologist might describe you differently, as a community. The human body contains about as many bacterial cells as human ones, most of them in the gut, and together they weigh about as much as the brain. This collection is known as the microbiome, and it's the subject of today's lecture.
C: For most of the twentieth century, medicine regarded bacteria almost entirely as enemies. That view has changed. The vast majority of our microbes are harmless, and many are essential.
C: What do they do for us? First, digestion. We can't break down many of the fibres in plants. Our gut bacteria can, and in the process they release substances that feed the cells lining the gut. Second, they manufacture certain vitamins. Third, simply by occupying the space, they prevent harmful species from settling.
C: And fourth, they train the immune system. A baby's immune system has to learn the difference between a threat and a friend, and it learns by meeting microbes. This may explain a striking pattern. Allergies and asthma have become far more common in wealthy countries. Children who grow up on farms, around animals, have much lower rates than children raised in cities.
C: Where do our microbes come from? The first arrive at birth, from the mother. More come from milk, from food, from pets and from soil. By the age of about three, a child's microbiome resembles an adult's.
C: Diet has a powerful influence. In a famous comparison, researchers studied children in a village in West Africa, who ate mainly grains and vegetables, and children in an Italian city. The African children had a far wider variety of gut bacteria. Variety seems to be a sign of health, and it's reduced by a diet that's low in fibre.
C: It's also reduced by antibiotics. These drugs save lives, and I'm not suggesting anyone refuse them. But they kill useful bacteria along with harmful ones, and after a course of treatment, the gut can take months to recover.
C: One of the most remarkable treatments to emerge from this research is the transplant of gut bacteria from a healthy donor. For one serious infection of the bowel, which kills thousands of patients in hospital every year, it cures about nine cases in ten, far more than any drug.
C: A caution to finish. You'll see claims that the microbiome explains everything from depression to obesity. Most of that evidence comes from experiments on mice, and it shows a link, not a cause. It's an exciting field, but a young one.
