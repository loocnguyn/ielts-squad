# R57 | Storing electricity
## Saving power for a windless night
[A] The cost of electricity from wind turbines and solar panels has fallen so far that in much of the world it is now cheaper than power from coal or gas. One awkward fact stands in the way of relying on it. The wind does not always blow, the sun sets every evening, and people want electricity at times that have nothing to do with either. An electricity grid must match supply and demand second by second. As the share of wind and solar power grows, the ability to store energy when it is plentiful and release it when it is scarce becomes the central problem of the whole system.
[B] Much the oldest and largest form of storage has nothing to do with batteries. In a pumped hydroelectric scheme, two reservoirs are built at different heights. When electricity is abundant and cheap, it is used to pump water uphill. When it is needed, the water is allowed to run back down through turbines. The first such plants were built in the Alps in the 1890s, and the method still accounts for the great majority of the world's storage capacity. About three quarters of the energy put in is recovered, and a large plant can deliver power for many hours. The limitation is geography. A scheme requires mountains, a great deal of water and the flooding of a valley, and in many countries the best sites have already been used.
[C] The fastest-growing technology is the lithium-ion battery, the same kind that powers phones and electric cars. Mass production for vehicles has cut its cost by about nine tenths in a decade, and installations the size of several football pitches, containing thousands of battery units in steel containers, are now connected to grids in Australia, California and China. Their great strength is speed. A battery can respond to a change in demand within a fraction of a second, far faster than any power station. Their weakness is duration. Most are designed to discharge for between two and four hours, which is enough to carry solar power from the afternoon into the evening but not to cover several still, cloudy days in winter.
[D] For longer periods, engineers are exploring a range of alternatives. In a flow battery, energy is held in two tanks of liquid, which are pumped past each other across a membrane. To store more, one simply builds bigger tanks, and the liquids do not wear out as the materials of a solid battery do. Another approach compresses air into underground caverns left by salt mining and releases it through a turbine. A plant of this kind has operated in Germany since 1978. More recent ideas use surplus electricity to heat sand or molten salt to several hundred degrees in insulated silos, or to raise and lower enormous weights.
[E] The hardest case is storage between seasons. In northern countries, solar panels produce most in summer, while demand peaks in winter. No battery could economically hold energy for six months. The leading candidate for this task is hydrogen, which can be made by using electricity to split water, kept in tanks or underground, and later burned in a turbine or passed through a fuel cell. The drawback is waste. By the time the electricity has been turned into hydrogen and back again, only about a third of the original energy remains. Supporters argue that this matters less than it seems if the electricity used would otherwise have been thrown away, as increasingly happens when wind farms are switched off because the grid cannot absorb their output.
[F] Some of the most promising storage is not on the grid at all. An electric car spends more than nine tenths of its life parked, with a battery far larger than a household needs in a day. With suitable equipment, cars could charge when power is plentiful and feed a little back at the evening peak. Trials in several countries have shown that the technology works. Whether owners will accept it, given concerns about wear on the battery, is another matter. A simpler step is to shift demand instead of supply. Water heaters, freezers and car chargers can be programmed to run when electricity is abundant, which reduces the amount of storage required in the first place.
[G] Storage also has costs beyond money. Lithium, cobalt and nickel must be mined, often in places where environmental rules are weak, and the extraction of lithium from salt flats in South America consumes large quantities of water in very dry regions. Recycling of batteries is technically possible but still on a small scale. Researchers are developing batteries based on sodium, which can be obtained from ordinary salt and is available everywhere, though at present they are heavier for the energy they hold, a disadvantage that matters little for a battery that sits in a field.
[H] No single technology is likely to prevail. The requirement is for a mixture, fast batteries to steady the grid from second to second, pumped water and other methods to shift energy across a day or a week, and perhaps hydrogen for the longest gaps, together with stronger cables to carry power from where the weather is favourable to where it is not. The engineering is largely understood. What remains uncertain is how quickly it can be built, and who will pay.

# R58 | Reading tree rings
## The calendar in the wood
[A] Cut through the trunk of a tree that has grown in a climate with seasons, and you will see a pattern of concentric rings. Each ring represents a year. In spring, when growth is rapid, the tree lays down large, thin-walled cells that appear pale. As the summer advances, the cells become smaller and denser, forming a darker band, and in winter growth stops altogether. The sharp boundary between one year's dark wood and the next year's pale wood marks the turn of the year. That the rings can be counted to find a tree's age has been known since ancient times. That they can be read as a detailed record of the past was the discovery of an American astronomer.
[B] Andrew Douglass was working at an observatory in Arizona in the early 1900s, studying the cycle of spots on the sun. He wondered whether the cycle affected the weather on earth and looked for a long record of climate with which to test the idea. It occurred to him that trees might provide one. In the dry south-west, a tree grows a wide ring in a wet year and a narrow one in a drought, and since all the trees in a district experience the same weather, they all show the same sequence of wide and narrow rings. He never did establish a clear link with sunspots. What he had found instead was a method of dating.
[C] The principle is known as cross-dating. The sequence of ring widths over several decades is as distinctive as a bar code. If the pattern in the outer rings of an old beam matches the pattern in the inner rings of a living tree whose dates are known, the two can be overlapped, and the calendar is extended back to the year in which the older tree began to grow. By joining together timbers of different ages in this way, researchers have built continuous sequences reaching back thousands of years. The longest, assembled from oaks preserved in the bogs and river gravels of central Europe, covers more than twelve thousand years.
[D] Douglass applied the method to a question that had long puzzled archaeologists, the age of the abandoned stone villages of the American south-west. Nobody knew whether they were five hundred or five thousand years old. By 1929 he had a sequence from living trees running back to about 1300, and a separate, floating sequence made from roof beams taken from the ruins, of unknown date. That summer, a charred log dug from a site in Arizona supplied the missing section and joined the two. At a stroke, some forty settlements could be assigned dates accurate to the year.
[E] The technique has since been put to a remarkable variety of uses. Historians have dated the timbers of cathedrals, ships and paintings on wooden panels, sometimes exposing forgeries. Because a precise date can be attached to each ring, wood of known age was used to check, and then to correct, the method of radiocarbon dating, which had been giving results that were too young by several centuries for the most ancient samples. Tree rings also record events. A fire leaves a scar that is later grown over. A year of intense cold may damage the cells and produce a so-called frost ring. A sequence of very narrow rings in trees across the northern hemisphere, beginning in the year 536, bears witness to a catastrophe, now attributed to massive volcanic eruptions, that dimmed the sun and caused harvests to fail from Ireland to China.
[F] The oldest individual trees are not, as might be supposed, the giants of the forest. They are the bristlecone pines of the White Mountains in California, small twisted trees that grow at an altitude of three thousand metres in poor soil and bitter cold. Some are more than four and a half thousand years old. Their slow growth produces wood so dense and so full of resin that it resists insects and decay, and dead trunks lie on the ground for thousands of years, allowing the record to be pushed back further still.
[G] It is rarely necessary to fell a tree to read it. A hollow drill, turned by hand, removes a core no thicker than a pencil from the bark to the centre, and the tree heals the wound. One notorious exception occurred in 1964, when a student researcher, whose drill had become stuck in a bristlecone pine in Nevada, was given permission to cut the tree down to recover it. When the rings were counted, the tree proved to have been nearly five thousand years old, and the oldest then known.
[H] Today the main use of tree rings is in the study of climate. Reliable measurements of temperature made with instruments go back little more than a century and a half. Rings allow that record to be extended over one or two thousand years, so that the warming of recent decades can be set against the natural variation of the past. The method has its difficulties. Growth responds to rainfall and to the age of the tree as well as to temperature, and separating these influences requires careful statistics. But combined with evidence from ice cores, corals and lake sediments, tree rings have shown that the present rate of warming has no parallel in the period they cover.

# R59 | What museums keep
## The hidden nine tenths
[A] A visitor walking through the galleries of a large museum might reasonably suppose that he or she is looking at the collection. In fact, what is on show is a tiny fraction of it. The Natural History Museum in London holds some eighty million specimens and displays far fewer than one in a hundred. The great art museums typically exhibit between two and five per cent of what they own. The rest is kept in basements, in warehouses on the edge of the city, and increasingly in purpose-built stores many miles away. To some critics this seems a scandal, a hoard of treasures locked away from the public that paid for them. People who work in museums see it differently.
[B] There are practical reasons why much cannot be shown. Many objects are too fragile. Watercolours, textiles, photographs and manuscripts fade irreversibly in light, and the usual rule is that such an item may be displayed for a few months and must then rest in darkness for several years. Others are of interest only to specialists, such as the ten thousand broken pieces of pottery from an excavation, each recorded and numbered, which taken together tell an archaeologist a great deal and which no visitor would wish to look at.
[C] In museums of natural history, the stored material is not a reserve for the galleries at all. It is the reason the institution exists. A drawer containing three hundred examples of the same species of beetle, collected in different places over two centuries, is a scientific instrument. By comparing them, a researcher can see how the insect varies, how its range has shifted and when it disappeared from a particular region. The specimen on which the first description of a species was based has a special status, and is kept permanently so that any later doubt about the identity of the species can be settled by returning to it.
[D] The value of such collections has grown with the techniques available for studying them. The collectors of the nineteenth century could not have imagined that DNA would one day be extracted from the skins of birds they had shot, or that the chemistry of a feather would reveal what the bird had been eating. Eggshells gathered by naturalists before 1940 provided the evidence, decades later, that the pesticide DDT was causing the shells of birds of prey to become thinner. Plants pressed and dried two hundred years ago are being used to measure how the timing of flowering has changed as the climate has warmed. No one can say what questions will be asked of the collections in another century, which is the strongest argument for keeping them.
[E] Storage is nevertheless a heavy burden. Rooms must be held at a steady temperature and humidity. Insects that eat wool, fur and feathers have to be kept at bay, formerly with poisons and now more often by freezing anything that enters the building. Every object needs a record, and in older institutions large parts of the collection have never been properly catalogued, so that curators do not know exactly what they have. Thefts have come to light years after the event because nobody had noticed that the items were missing.
[F] This raises the question of whether museums should ever dispose of things. In many countries the idea is close to taboo. Donors give objects in the belief that they will be kept for ever, and a museum that sold a painting to pay for repairs to its roof would find future gifts drying up. Professional codes generally permit disposal only when an object is a duplicate, damaged beyond use, or outside the museum's field, and require that it be offered first to another public collection. Those in favour of a looser approach point out that collections cannot grow indefinitely, and that an object lying unseen and unstudied in one museum might be the centrepiece of another.
[G] A different response is to open the stores themselves. A number of museums have built storage facilities that visitors can enter, walking between glass-fronted racks while conservators work in view. In Rotterdam, a museum of art has constructed a building shaped like a bowl and covered in mirrors, in which its entire holdings of more than a hundred and fifty thousand works are kept on shelves and sliding screens that the public can tour. Supporters say that this makes plain how a museum actually operates. Sceptics reply that looking at racks of unlabelled objects through glass is not the same as understanding them.
[H] The most far-reaching change is digital. Museums are photographing their collections and publishing the images and records online, where anyone may search them. A specialist in Brazil can now examine a beetle held in Berlin without leaving her desk, and several institutions have invited volunteers to help by transcribing the handwritten labels on millions of specimens. The work is slow and costly, and only a small proportion has been done. But it holds out the prospect that the hidden nine tenths will, for the first time, be visible to all.

# R60 | The body clock
## Living against the clock
[A] In 1729, a French astronomer named Jean-Jacques de Mairan performed an experiment on a plant. He had noticed that the leaves of a mimosa open towards the sun each morning and fold at dusk, and he wondered what would happen if the plant were deprived of light. He shut one in a dark cupboard and found that, for several days, the leaves went on opening and closing at the usual times. The plant, it appeared, did not simply respond to the sun. It carried a clock of its own.
[B] We now know that almost every living thing does the same, from bacteria to human beings. These internal rhythms are called circadian, from the Latin for about a day, and the name is exact. People who volunteer to live for weeks in caves or windowless rooms, without any clue to the time, continue to sleep and wake in a regular cycle, but it drifts. For most, the natural period is slightly longer than twenty-four hours. In ordinary life, the clock is corrected each day by light, and above all by the light of morning.
[C] The machinery was worked out in the late twentieth century, largely through experiments on fruit flies, and it earned three American scientists a Nobel Prize in 2017. Inside each cell, certain genes direct the production of proteins which gradually build up, and which, on reaching a high enough level, switch off the genes that made them. The proteins then decay, the genes switch on again, and the cycle repeats, taking about a day to complete. In mammals, the countless clocks in the cells of the body are kept in step by a cluster of some twenty thousand nerve cells at the base of the brain, directly above the point where the nerves from the eyes cross.
[D] The clock governs far more than sleep. Body temperature falls to its lowest in the hours before dawn and peaks in the early evening. Blood pressure rises sharply on waking. The hormone that prepares the body for sleep is released as darkness falls, and the liver, the gut and the immune system all follow daily schedules. One consequence is that the time at which a medicine is taken can alter its effect, and trials have shown that some drugs for cancer and for blood pressure work better, or do less harm, at particular hours.
[E] For most of human history, the clock and the day were in agreement. Artificial light has changed that. The signal that resets the clock is carried by special cells in the eye that are most sensitive to blue light, of the kind that is abundant in daylight and also in the glow of screens and modern lamps. Bright light in the evening tells the brain that it is still day, delays the release of the sleep hormone and pushes the whole cycle later. At the same time, people who spend their days indoors receive far less morning light than their ancestors did. The result is a clock that runs late and is only weakly set.
[F] The people most severely affected are those who work at night. About one worker in five in industrial countries does some form of shift work. Their clocks rarely adapt fully, since on days off they return to a daytime routine, and they are obliged to be alert when their bodies are prepared for sleep and to sleep when they are prepared for activity. Large studies of nurses and factory workers have linked long periods of night work with higher rates of diabetes, heart disease and some cancers, and accidents at work and on the road are more frequent in the early hours.
[G] A milder version of the same conflict affects a much larger group. People differ in their natural timing. Some are early types, alert at dawn, and others late types, who come to life in the evening. The tendency is partly inherited, and it shifts with age, reaching its latest point at around nineteen or twenty. Teenagers who cannot fall asleep before midnight and cannot wake for school are not being lazy. Their clocks have moved. Late types forced to rise early on working days and sleeping late at weekends live in a state that researchers have named social jet lag, as if they flew across several time zones every Friday and back every Monday. A number of schools that have experimented with a later start to the day have reported better attendance and improved results.
[H] The practical advice that follows from all this is simple, though not always easy to act on. Seek daylight early in the day, preferably outdoors. Keep lights low and screens dim in the last hours of the evening. Go to bed and get up at roughly the same times, including at weekends. And for those who design timetables for schools, hospitals and factories, there is a larger lesson. The human body is not a machine that performs equally at any hour, and arrangements that ignore its rhythms carry a cost.
