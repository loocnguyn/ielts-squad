# R33 | The Great Fire of London
## The fire that remade a city
[A] In the early hours of Sunday 2 September 1666, a fire broke out in the bakery of Thomas Farriner in Pudding Lane, close to London Bridge. Fires were common in the city, and when the Lord Mayor was woken and brought to look at it, he was unimpressed and went back to bed. By the time it burned itself out four days later, it had destroyed more than thirteen thousand houses, eighty-seven churches and the medieval cathedral of St Paul's. Something like four fifths of the area within the city walls lay in ashes.
[B] London had been a disaster waiting to happen. Its houses were built largely of timber, with upper storeys that jutted out over narrow lanes until they almost touched those opposite. Many roofs were thatched, in defiance of regulations that had forbidden thatch for centuries. Warehouses along the river were packed with oil, tar, spirits and timber. The summer of 1666 had been exceptionally hot and dry, and on the night the fire began a strong wind was blowing from the east, driving the flames towards the heart of the city.
[C] The methods available for fighting it were primitive. There was no fire service. Each parish kept leather buckets, axes and long hooks, and the water supply ran through wooden pipes that had to be dug up and broken open. The only effective technique was to pull down houses in the path of the flames to create a gap. The Lord Mayor hesitated to order this, since the law made him liable for the cost of rebuilding any house destroyed on his instructions. By the time the king overruled him, the fire had grown too fierce to approach. It was finally halted on the Wednesday, when the wind dropped and sailors from the royal dockyards used gunpowder to blow up whole rows of houses.
[D] Remarkably few deaths were recorded, fewer than ten according to the official figures, though some historians suspect the true number was higher, since the poor were not always counted and the heat was intense enough to leave no remains. The human cost lay elsewhere. Around seventy thousand people were made homeless and camped for months in the fields to the north. In the search for someone to blame, mobs attacked foreigners, and a French watchmaker who confessed to starting the fire was hanged, although it later emerged that he had not arrived in the country until after it began.
[E] Within days, several ambitious plans for a new city had been laid before the king. The most famous was by Christopher Wren, who proposed sweeping away the tangle of medieval lanes in favour of broad, straight avenues meeting at open squares, in the manner of Paris or Rome. None of the plans was adopted. Thousands of owners held rights over small plots of land, and sorting out their claims in order to redraw the map would have taken years, while merchants were anxious to resume trading before their business moved elsewhere. Houses went up again along the old lines, and the street plan of the City of London today is still, in essentials, the medieval one.
[F] If the layout stayed the same, the buildings did not. An Act of Parliament passed in 1667 laid down rules that transformed the appearance of the city. All new houses were to be of brick or stone. Their height was fixed according to the width of the street, in four classes. Upper floors could no longer project, and the principal streets were widened. Surveyors were appointed to inspect the work. These were among the first comprehensive building regulations in Europe, and they were paid for by a tax on the coal brought into London by sea.
[G] The fire had less obvious consequences as well. Disputes between landlords and tenants over who should pay for rebuilding were so numerous that a special court was created to settle them quickly, and it sat for nine years. A physician turned speculative builder, Nicholas Barbon, saw a commercial opportunity and in 1680 set up an office that, in return for an annual payment, undertook to rebuild a client's house if it burned. He also employed his own teams of men to put fires out, and marked the houses he insured with a metal badge. This was the beginning of the fire insurance industry, and, since the insurers' brigades were for more than a century the only organised firefighters in London, of the fire service too.
[H] One popular belief about the fire deserves a brief mention. It is often said that the flames ended the Great Plague, which had killed perhaps a fifth of London's population the year before, by destroying the rats and the filthy housing that harboured it. The timing makes this unlikely. The epidemic was already fading before the fire, and the districts where it had been worst were the poor suburbs outside the walls, which the flames never reached.

# R34 | Procrastination
## Why we put things off
[A] Almost everyone delays tasks from time to time. For a substantial minority, estimated at around a fifth of adults, the habit is persistent and damaging. They pay bills late, put off visits to the doctor, and submit work at the last minute or not at all. Surveys of university students suggest that most of them regard procrastination as a problem, and it has been linked with lower marks, poorer health and reduced earnings. The puzzling feature of the behaviour is that people do it knowingly. To procrastinate is not simply to postpone, which may be perfectly sensible. It is to postpone something while expecting to be worse off as a result.
[B] The usual explanation, offered by procrastinators themselves as readily as by their critics, is poor time management or plain laziness. Neither stands up well. Procrastinators are often busy, tidying their desks or answering unimportant messages, while the dreaded task waits. And courses that teach planning and scheduling have a disappointing record. They help people who are disorganised, but they do little for those who know exactly what they should be doing and still do not do it.
[C] Economists have approached the problem through the way people value the future. Offered a choice between a sum of money in a year and a slightly larger sum in a year and a day, nearly everyone is content to wait the extra day. Offered the same choice between today and tomorrow, many take the smaller sum at once. A reward that is immediate looms disproportionately large. The same is true of costs. The effort of starting a report is felt now, while the benefit of finishing it lies weeks away, and so the balance tips towards delay. The trouble is that when tomorrow arrives, it has become today, and the same reasoning applies again.
[D] Psychologists have added an emotional dimension. In their account, procrastination is less a failure to manage time than a failure to manage mood. A task that provokes anxiety, boredom or self-doubt is unpleasant to think about, and avoiding it brings instant relief. That relief is a reward, and behaviour that is rewarded tends to be repeated. The cost comes later, in guilt and in the stress of a looming deadline, which make the task more unpleasant still and the urge to escape it stronger. Brain imaging studies offer some support, having found that habitual procrastinators show differences in regions involved in regulating emotion.
[E] This helps to explain a finding that surprises many people. It might be expected that those who are hardest on themselves would be the least likely to delay. The reverse appears to be true. In a study of students preparing for examinations, those who forgave themselves for having procrastinated before the first test procrastinated less before the second. Harsh self-criticism seems to add to the negative feelings that drive avoidance, whereas treating a lapse with some kindness makes it easier to face the task again.
[F] A fascinating series of experiments suggests that part of the problem lies in how we think about our future selves. When people are asked to imagine themselves ten years from now while their brains are scanned, the pattern of activity resembles that produced by thinking about a stranger more than that produced by thinking about themselves. If the person who will suffer from our delay feels like someone else, it is not surprising that we are willing to hand them the burden. In one study, volunteers who were shown digitally aged photographs of their own faces subsequently chose to put more money into a savings account for retirement.
[G] What, then, actually helps? The evidence favours methods that reduce the emotional cost of beginning. One is to shrink the first step until it no longer provokes resistance, committing oneself only to write a single sentence or to work for five minutes. Starting is usually the hardest part, and once under way most people continue. Another is to decide in advance exactly when and where a task will be done, in the form of a specific plan. A third is to remove temptations beforehand, on the principle that it is easier to avoid a distraction than to resist it. Deadlines imposed by someone else are more effective than those we set ourselves. In a well-known experiment, students who were given three evenly spaced deadlines for three essays did better than those who were allowed to hand in all three at the end of term, and also better than those who chose their own dates.
[H] It is worth adding that not every delay is a vice. Some writers have praised what they call structured procrastination, in which a person avoids one task by completing others that are useful. And there is evidence that leaving a creative problem unfinished for a while can improve the eventual solution, since the mind continues to work on it in the background. The difficulty, as always, lies in telling a productive pause from an excuse.

# R35 | The green Sahara
## When the desert was a grassland
[A] In the heart of the Sahara, hundreds of kilometres from the nearest permanent water, there are cliffs covered with paintings and carvings. They show giraffes, elephants and hippopotamuses, herds of cattle, and in one remote cave a row of small human figures that appear to be swimming. Bones of crocodiles and fish hooks carved from bone have been dug from the sand nearby. The people who made these images were not imagining another country. They were recording the world around them. Between roughly eleven thousand and five thousand years ago, the largest hot desert on earth was a land of lakes, rivers and grass.
[B] The cause lay far away, in the motion of the earth itself. The planet's axis is not fixed but wobbles slowly, like a spinning top, completing a circle about every twenty-three thousand years. The effect is to change the season in which each hemisphere is closest to the sun. Eleven thousand years ago, the northern summer coincided with the earth's nearest approach, so that summers in North Africa received noticeably more sunlight than they do now. The land heated more strongly, the air above it rose, and moist air was drawn in from the Atlantic to replace it. The belt of summer rain that today barely reaches the southern fringe of the desert moved a thousand kilometres to the north.
[C] The change in sunlight alone, however, was too small to account for the transformation. Climate models that included nothing else could not make the Sahara green enough to match the evidence. The missing element was the land's own response. Bare sand is pale and reflects most of the sunlight that falls on it. Grass and shrubs are darker, absorb more heat, and return water to the air through their leaves. Once plants began to spread, they warmed and moistened the air above them, which strengthened the rains, which allowed more plants to grow. A modest push from the sun was magnified by the vegetation into a new climate.
[D] The evidence for that climate is abundant. Radar images taken from space reveal the channels of great rivers buried under the dunes. Lake Chad, which today is shallow and shrinking, was then an inland sea larger than any lake on earth now, and its ancient shoreline can still be traced. Cores of mud drilled from the floor of the Atlantic, off the coast of West Africa, provide a continuous record. In the layers laid down during the humid period, there is far less of the dust that winds now carry out from the desert, and far more of the chemical traces left by leafy plants.
[E] How the wet period ended has been a matter of dispute. The slow change in the earth's orbit that had brought the rains gradually took them away again, over several thousand years. Yet the ocean cores off the north-west coast appear to show the supply of dust rising sharply within a few centuries, about five and a half thousand years ago, as though the system had flipped. Records from lakes further east tell a different story, of a decline spread over millennia. The likeliest explanation is that both are right, and that the drying was sudden in some regions and slow in others, depending on local conditions.
[F] Some researchers have suggested that people played a part. By this time the inhabitants were herding cattle, sheep and goats, and heavy grazing may have stripped away vegetation in areas that were already under stress, hastening the return of bare ground. Others argue the opposite, that herders, by moving their animals with the seasons, would have helped to keep the grassland in balance, and that the collapse came from the climate alone. The question is unresolved.
[G] What is not in doubt is the effect on the people. As the grassland failed, they moved towards the few places where water remained, above all the valley of the Nile. It is surely no coincidence that the first settled kingdoms of Egypt arose in the centuries after the Sahara dried, when a growing population was crowded into a narrow strip of fertile land and had to organise itself to share it.
[H] The cycle continues, and in the ordinary course of things the Sahara would turn green again in ten thousand years or so. Whether human influence on the climate will alter that timetable is unknown. Some models suggest that a warmer world will bring more rain to the southern edge of the desert, and satellite images over recent decades do show parts of that zone becoming greener. But the lesson of the past is one of caution. It shows that the desert's border is not fixed, and that a small change can be amplified by the land into a very large one, in either direction.

# R36 | Citizen science
## Science by the many
[A] Every winter since 1900, on a single day around Christmas, volunteers across North America have gone out to count the birds they can see within a set area. The custom was begun by an ornithologist who wanted to replace a traditional holiday hunt with something less destructive. More than a century later, the count involves tens of thousands of people, and its records form the longest continuous survey of wildlife in existence. Scientists have used them to show how the ranges of hundreds of species have shifted northwards as winters have warmed. No team of professionals could have gathered such data, and no research budget could have paid for it.
[B] The participation of amateurs in research is not new. Until the late nineteenth century, most science was done by people who were not paid for it, and Charles Darwin himself held no university post. What has changed is the scale, and the cause is technology. A smartphone can record a photograph, the time and an exact location, and transmit all three in seconds. On one popular platform, users upload pictures of plants and animals, software suggests an identification, and other users confirm or correct it. The platform has accumulated well over a hundred million records, among them species previously unknown to science and others that had been presumed extinct.
[C] A second kind of project needs no fieldwork at all. Modern instruments produce more data than scientists can examine. In 2007, astronomers at Oxford, faced with images of a million galaxies that had to be sorted by shape, put them on a website and invited the public to help. They expected the task to take years. Within twenty-four hours, visitors were classifying fifty thousand images an hour. Because each galaxy was shown to many people, mistakes by individuals were outvoted, and the combined result proved as reliable as the judgment of experts. One participant, a Dutch schoolteacher, noticed a strange green object that fitted none of the categories. It turned out to be a kind of gas cloud that nobody had seen before, and it now bears her name.
[D] A third approach turns research into a game. Working out how a protein folds into its three-dimensional shape is a notoriously hard problem, and one at which people's intuition about space can outperform a computer's calculation. Biochemists in Seattle designed a puzzle in which players twist and tuck a protein on screen and earn points for more stable arrangements. In 2011, players of this game determined the structure of an enzyme from a virus related to HIV in three weeks, a problem that had defeated researchers for fifteen years.
[E] The obvious objection is that data collected by untrained people cannot be trusted. Studies that have checked suggest that the concern is real but manageable. Volunteers tend to be good at spotting common, distinctive species and poor at rare or similar-looking ones. They visit attractive places at weekends in fine weather, so that records are thin for remote areas, weekdays and winter. Well-designed projects allow for this, by providing training, by asking several volunteers to examine the same item, and by using statistical methods that correct for uneven effort. Where these safeguards are in place, comparisons have generally found the quality of amateur data to be close to that of professionals.
[F] There are deeper questions about who takes part and who benefits. Surveys indicate that volunteers are far from representative of the population. They tend to be older, wealthier and more highly educated than average. And the relationship can be one-sided. Volunteers supply their time and observations, while the questions are set, and the papers written, by academics. Critics have asked whether such arrangements deserve to be called participation at all, and some projects have been accused of using the public as free labour.
[G] A different model has emerged in response, in which communities decide for themselves what needs investigating. Residents of a district near an oil refinery in the United States, worried about their health and dissatisfied with official monitoring, learned to take air samples with simple equipment made from plastic buckets. Their results revealed levels of pollution that the authorities' instruments had missed, and eventually forced stricter controls. After the nuclear accident at Fukushima in Japan in 2011, volunteers who distrusted official figures built their own radiation detectors, fixed them to cars, and produced a public map more detailed than the government's.
[H] Enthusiasts sometimes claim that citizen science will transform the relationship between researchers and society. That may be too much to expect. Most volunteers contribute once or twice and are never heard from again, and a small core does the bulk of the work. But there is good evidence that taking part improves people's understanding of how science operates, including its uncertainties. At a time when trust in expertise cannot be taken for granted, that may prove to be as valuable as the data.
