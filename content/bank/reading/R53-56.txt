# R53 | Fermentation
## The oldest biotechnology
[A] Bread, cheese, yoghurt, wine, beer, soy sauce, vinegar, chocolate and coffee have something in common. All of them owe their character to the activity of microbes. Fermentation, the process by which yeasts and bacteria transform the sugars in food, is among the oldest technologies that human beings possess, and it was practised for thousands of years by people who had no idea that microbes existed. It has recently returned to fashion, promoted for its supposed benefits to health. The history is remarkable enough without exaggeration.
[B] The origins are lost, but they were almost certainly accidental. Fruit left in a container ferments by itself, since wild yeasts live on its skin, and grain that has got wet and begun to sprout will do the same. Chemical traces of a fermented drink made from rice, honey and fruit have been identified on fragments of pottery from a Chinese village nine thousand years old. Some archaeologists have gone further and proposed that the desire for beer, and not for bread, was what first persuaded hunters and gatherers in the Middle East to settle and cultivate cereals. The suggestion is impossible to prove, but large stone vessels apparently used for brewing have been found at a site in Turkey that is older than any known farm.
[C] Whatever its beginnings, fermentation spread because it solved a practical problem, which was how to keep food from spoiling. Milk turns sour within a day in a warm climate. Made into cheese, it will last for months or years. Cabbage salted and packed into a barrel becomes sauerkraut, and remains edible through the winter. The principle is the same in each case. The harmless bacteria that carry out the fermentation produce acid or alcohol as a by-product, and in doing so create conditions in which the organisms that cause decay and disease cannot survive. In effect, people learned to defend their food by encouraging one group of microbes to drive out the rest.
[D] There were other advantages, less obvious to those who benefited from them. Fermentation can make food more nourishing. The process that turns soya beans into tempeh, in Indonesia, breaks down substances in the raw bean that interfere with digestion. Milk is a striking case. Most adults in the world cannot easily digest the sugar it contains, but in yoghurt and aged cheese much of that sugar has already been consumed by bacteria. Fermentation also removes poisons. Cassava, a root that is the staple food of several hundred million people in the tropics, contains compounds that release cyanide, and it must be soaked and fermented before it is safe to eat.
[E] For most of history, the process was a mystery. Brewers knew that the froth from one batch would start the next, without knowing what the froth was. In the 1850s, the French chemist Louis Pasteur was asked by a manufacturer of alcohol from beetroot to find out why some of his vats were turning sour. Looking through a microscope, Pasteur saw that the healthy vats contained round yeast cells, while the spoiled ones swarmed with smaller, rod-shaped organisms. He concluded that fermentation was not a purely chemical reaction, as the leading scientists of the day maintained, but the work of living things, and that different organisms produced different results. The insight led directly to his later work on infectious disease.
[F] Industrialisation changed the practice profoundly. Where bakers and brewers had relied on whatever mixture of microbes lived in their premises, manufacturers now used pure strains grown in laboratories, which gave a consistent product every time. Refrigeration and canning took over the task of preservation. Many traditional fermented foods were replaced by quicker imitations. Most supermarket pickles, for instance, are simply vegetables in vinegar, and much commercial bread is raised in an hour or two, where a traditional loaf took a day.
[G] The current revival is driven largely by interest in the bacteria that inhabit the human gut. Fermented foods that have not been heated after fermentation contain large numbers of live microbes, and enthusiasts claim that eating them improves digestion, immunity and even mood. The evidence is mixed. A study at Stanford University in 2021 found that volunteers who ate several servings of fermented foods a day for ten weeks had a greater variety of bacteria in their gut and lower levels of certain markers of inflammation. That is encouraging, but it was a small study, and whether such changes lead to better health in the long run has not been shown. Most of the microbes in fermented foods do not settle permanently in the gut. They pass through.
[H] Meanwhile, fermentation in a broader sense has become one of the most important industrial processes in the world. The citric acid in soft drinks, many vitamins, and antibiotics such as penicillin are produced by microbes grown in steel tanks. Since the 1980s, bacteria and yeasts have been genetically altered to manufacture substances they would never make in nature, including the insulin used by people with diabetes, which was formerly extracted from the organs of pigs. The technology of the brewer's vat, discovered by accident before history began, now produces a large share of the world's medicines.

# R54 | Open-plan offices
## The office without walls
[A] Walk into the headquarters of almost any large company today and you will find the same arrangement. Rows of desks stretch across a floor without partitions, and managers sit among their staff. The open-plan office has become so universal that it is hard to remember it was once a radical experiment. Its promoters promised that it would transform the way people work together. The evidence that has accumulated over half a century suggests that it has done so, though not in the way they intended.
[B] The idea was born in Germany in the late 1950s, when two brothers who ran a consultancy near Hamburg proposed what they called the office landscape. They believed that the traditional layout, with clerks in rows and executives behind closed doors, obstructed the flow of information. In their design, desks were grouped in irregular clusters according to who needed to talk to whom, separated by plants and curved screens, and nobody had a private room. The intention was democratic as well as practical.
[C] The concept crossed the Atlantic and was soon altered. In 1964, an American designer, Robert Propst, introduced a system of movable panels and work surfaces that could be arranged to give each employee a semi-enclosed space, with room to spread out papers and to stand as well as sit. He intended it to be flexible and humane. Companies discovered that the panels could be used to pack the largest possible number of people into a floor, each in an identical box. The cubicle, as it became known, was born, and Propst spent his later years lamenting what had been done with his invention.
[D] In the 1990s and 2000s, technology firms in California led a reaction against the cubicle, tearing down the panels and returning to open floors, now with bright colours, sofas and table football. The stated reason was collaboration. Chance meetings, it was argued, spark ideas, and walls prevent them. A less publicised reason was cost. An open floor holds far more desks than one divided into rooms, and property is among the largest expenses of any business in a city.
[E] The complaints of those who work in such offices have been documented in survey after survey. The most frequent concerns noise, and in particular other people's conversations. Experiments show that background speech is far more disruptive than mechanical noise of the same volume, because the brain cannot help trying to follow it. After an interruption, it can take many minutes to regain full concentration on a demanding task. Lack of privacy comes next. People who feel that they are constantly observed report higher stress, and studies in Scandinavia have found that employees in open offices take more days of sick leave than those with rooms of their own, a difference attributed partly to the spread of infections and partly to strain.
[F] These drawbacks might be worth bearing if the promised collaboration materialised. The most rigorous test of that claim was published in 2018 by two researchers at Harvard. They studied two large companies that were about to move staff from cubicles to fully open floors. For several weeks before and after the change, volunteers wore electronic badges that recorded, by means of sensors, when they were in conversation with a colleague, and the researchers also counted their emails and instant messages. The outcome was the opposite of what the companies had hoped. Face-to-face interaction fell by about seventy per cent, while electronic messages increased. Exposed to view on every side, people had put on headphones, avoided eye contact and typed to colleagues sitting a few metres away.
[G] The researchers' explanation was that human beings need some control over their encounters. When a boundary is removed, they construct another, and a fourth wall, as they called it, of polite indifference is harder to penetrate than a door that can be knocked on. Later studies have refined the picture. Small open offices shared by a team of five or six seem to work reasonably well. It is the very large, undivided floor that produces withdrawal.
[H] Designers have responded with a compromise that goes by various names. Staff are offered a range of settings, quiet rooms for concentrated work, booths for telephone calls, tables for meetings and open areas for casual conversation, and move between them as their tasks require. Often no one has a desk of their own. Whether this is an improvement or merely a further saving on space is disputed, and many employees dislike beginning each day by hunting for somewhere to sit. The spread of working from home has changed the question once again. If people can concentrate better in their own kitchens, the purpose of the office may come to be precisely the conversation that the open plan was supposed to encourage, and failed to.

# R55 | Lake Baikal
## The oldest lake on earth
[A] Most lakes are short-lived features of the landscape. They form in hollows left by glaciers or in the bends of rivers, and within ten or twenty thousand years they fill with sediment and vanish. Lake Baikal, in the south of Siberia, is a spectacular exception. It is at least twenty-five million years old, and it is still growing. It is also the deepest lake in the world, reaching more than sixteen hundred metres, and although several lakes cover a larger area, none holds more water. Baikal contains about a fifth of all the unfrozen fresh water on the surface of the planet, more than the five Great Lakes of North America combined.
[B] The explanation for its age and depth lies in its origin. Baikal occupies a rift, a place where the crust of the earth is being pulled apart. The two sides are separating by a few millimetres a year, and the floor between them is sinking. Beneath the water lies a layer of sediment as much as seven kilometres thick, which has accumulated without interruption since the lake began. For geologists, a core drilled from this mud is an archive of the climate of central Asia over millions of years.
[C] A lake so old has had time to evolve a fauna of its own. Of the two and a half thousand or so species of animal recorded in Baikal, around two thirds are found nowhere else. They include more than three hundred kinds of small shrimp-like creatures, some of them brightly coloured and as long as a finger, which between them consume much of the dead matter that falls to the bottom. There is a fish, the golomyanka, that has no scales, is nearly transparent, and gives birth to live young instead of laying eggs. A third of its body weight is oil, and when one is washed ashore and left in the sun, it is said to melt away, leaving only bones.
[D] The most famous inhabitant is the Baikal seal, the only species of seal in the world that lives exclusively in fresh water. How its ancestors arrived, several thousand kilometres from the nearest ocean, has long been debated. The most probable route is from the Arctic, up the rivers that drain northwards, at a time during the ice ages when ice sheets blocked those rivers and created great lakes along their course.
[E] Baikal is renowned for the clarity of its water. In early summer, an object can be seen at a depth of forty metres. This is largely the work of a single animal, a tiny crustacean no bigger than a grain of rice, which is present in enormous numbers and filters bacteria and algae from the water. It is exquisitely adapted to cold and cannot survive in water much warmer than fifteen degrees. Another unusual feature is that the lake is rich in oxygen all the way to the bottom. In most deep lakes the lower layers are stagnant, but in Baikal cold surface water sinks each spring and autumn and carries oxygen down with it, so that animals live at every depth.
[F] For five months of the year, the lake is frozen. The ice may be more than a metre thick, strong enough to carry lorries, and so clear in places that it is possible to look down through it into the water. In the winter of 1904, during a war with Japan, the Russian army laid a railway track across the frozen surface in order to move troops eastwards, since the line around the southern shore had not yet been completed.
[G] The lake's isolation protected it for most of its history, but it now faces several threats. For nearly half a century, a mill on the southern shore that produced paper pulp discharged its waste into the water. It was finally closed in 2013, though millions of tonnes of toxic sludge remain in ponds beside the lake. Sewage from growing towns and from tourist developments has encouraged blankets of green algae along parts of the shore, an alarming sight in a lake famous for its purity, and in the same areas large numbers of the sponges that grow on the lake bed have died. The river that supplies half of Baikal's water flows in from Mongolia, where plans to build hydroelectric dams have caused concern.
[H] The gravest threat may be the one that is hardest to address. The region around Baikal is warming considerably faster than the world as a whole. The period of ice cover has shortened by about two weeks over the past century, and the surface water in summer is measurably warmer. Many of the lake's unique species, like the little crustacean that keeps the water clear, have evolved over millions of years in constant cold. Animals that have never had to adapt to change may be poorly equipped to do so, and those that cannot have nowhere else to go.

# R56 | The invention of zero
## Nothing matters
[A] It is difficult to think of a symbol that looks less impressive than a small empty circle. Yet the invention of zero has been called one of the greatest achievements of the human mind, and for most of history most civilisations managed without it. The Greeks, who laid the foundations of geometry, had no zero. Nor did the Romans, whose numerals are still used on clock faces and at the end of films. The idea arrived in Europe only in the Middle Ages, and when it did, it was met in some quarters with hostility.
[B] To see why zero matters, it helps to distinguish two of its roles. The first is as a placeholder. In our system of writing numbers, the value of a digit depends on its position. The 2 in 25 means twenty, and the 2 in 250 means two hundred. For such a system to work, there must be a way of showing that a position is empty, so that 205 can be told apart from 25. The second role is as a number in its own right, a quantity that can be added, subtracted and multiplied like any other. The first idea occurred to several peoples independently. The second was far rarer.
[C] The Babylonians of ancient Iraq used a positional system based on sixty, traces of which survive in our minutes and seconds. For many centuries they simply left a gap where a position was empty, which led to obvious confusion, and by about 300 BC their scribes had begun to insert a pair of slanted wedges as a marker. They never used it at the end of a number, however, and never treated it as a quantity. On the other side of the world, the Maya of Central America devised a symbol for zero, often drawn as a shell, for use in their elaborate calendar. It appears to have had no influence beyond their own culture.
[D] The decisive step was taken in India. A dot used as a placeholder appears in an Indian manuscript on birch bark, parts of which have been dated to as early as the third or fourth century. And in the year 628, the astronomer Brahmagupta wrote a treatise in which, for the first time that we know of, zero is discussed as a number. He set out rules for calculating with it. A number minus itself is zero. A number plus zero is unchanged. A number multiplied by zero is zero. He also dealt with what we would call negative numbers, which he described as debts, in contrast to fortunes. On division by zero he was less successful, and the question was to trouble mathematicians for another thousand years.
[E] From India the system travelled west. In the ninth century, the Persian scholar al-Khwarizmi, working in Baghdad, wrote a book explaining the Indian method of calculation. The Arabic word for the empty place, sifr, is the origin of both the English words zero and cipher, and the Latin form of al-Khwarizmi's own name has given us the word algorithm. The numerals reached Europe by way of Spain and Italy. Their most effective champion was Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, the son of a merchant, who had learned the system as a boy in North Africa. In a book published in 1202, he showed how much faster and more reliable it made the keeping of accounts than the Roman numerals and counting boards then in use.
[F] Adoption was slow nonetheless. In 1299, the city of Florence forbade bankers to use the new numerals in their records. The objection was not superstition, as is sometimes said, but fear of fraud. A zero could easily be turned into a six or a nine with a stroke of the pen, or added to the end of a figure to multiply it by ten, whereas Roman numerals were harder to alter. Merchants went on using the numerals privately, since they were so much more convenient, and with the spread of printing in the fifteenth century, which fixed the shapes of the digits, resistance faded.
[G] There was an intellectual obstacle as well. The philosophy inherited from Aristotle held that nothingness could not exist, and a symbol for nothing seemed to some scholars to be a contradiction. Yet it was precisely the willingness to calculate with zero, and with quantities approaching it, that opened the way to the mathematics of the seventeenth century. The calculus developed by Newton and Leibniz depends on reasoning about what happens as a quantity becomes vanishingly small. Without it there would be no modern physics or engineering.
[H] The latest chapter is the most familiar. Every digital computer represents information as a pattern of two states, written as one and zero. Each photograph, song and message stored on a phone is, at bottom, an immense string of those two digits. A concept that entire civilisations did without, and that others regarded with suspicion, has become half of the alphabet in which the modern world is written.
